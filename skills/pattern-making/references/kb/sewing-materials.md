# Sewing techniques, stitches, machines, fabrics and materials knowledge base

Entry-per-item database for couture and tailoring techniques, ISO stitch and seam classes, machines and
feet, fabric-specific seam and hem choices, and materials (fibres, weaves, knits, interfacings, notions,
pressing). Format: `FORMAT.md`. The broad sheet `../techniques/sewing-techniques.md` (cited as ST) already
holds the seam, hem, closure and pressing tables; this file goes deeper and does not repeat them. The knit
sheet `../techniques/knits-lingerie-swimwear.md` is cited as KL (stretch classes, band ratios, swim ease).

How to read the entries:
- These entries are techniques and materials, not garments. **Pieces** lists what the technique needs in the
  pattern (extra pieces, strips, tapes, facings). **Shape** states what the technique does to the pattern
  geometry (allowance, grain, length of an edge, ease, anchors). No entry was taken from a drawn pattern
  outline; every Shape field is "unverified" as an outline and states trade practice. Check on a toile in the
  real fabric.
- Numbers are typical working values in cm or mm. "Typical" means a range to confirm on a scrap of the real
  fabric. Confidence is given per entry: "trade practice" = consistent across the usual tailoring, couture and
  industrial references; "from memory" = recalled, not re-checked online; "verified" = checked on the web in
  this session (see Sources).
- Anchors used: NP neck point, SP shoulder point, UP underarm, CF centre front, CB centre back, BP break point
  (where a lapel starts to fold), AH armhole, WL waist line, SA seam allowance.

## Hand-basting {#hand-basting}

- **Aliases**: tacking, thread basting, even basting, uneven basting, diagonal basting, slip basting; fr: faufilage, bâti à la main, bâti diagonal
- **Family**: couture and tailoring techniques
- **Recognize**: long loose running stitches in a contrasting soft cotton thread along seam lines, around pockets, on lapels and around sleeve caps of unfinished couture or tailored garments; removed after the permanent stitching
- **Pieces**: no extra piece. Needs matched notches, marked seam lines (thread tracing or tailor's tacks) and a SA wide enough to hold the basting without crossing the stitching line (1.5 to 2.5 cm)
- **Shape**: does not change the outline. It fixes the fitting order: pieces are joined along the marked net line, not along the cut edge, so the pattern must carry notches and drill marks on the net line. Source: unverified (trade practice).
- **Drafting**:
  1. Add notches at every match point (typical: every 15 to 20 cm on long seams, plus all ease limits, dart ends, pocket and pleat points).
  2. Mark the net line on the fabric by thread tracing (tailor's tacks, 1 to 2 cm stitches) rather than chalk on delicate cloth.
  3. Choose the stitch: even basting 6 to 12 mm (seams, joining layers; typical), uneven basting (long stitch on top 2 to 2.5 cm, short 5 to 6 mm underneath; holds flat layers over long distances), diagonal basting (rows of parallel slanted stitches 2 to 3 cm apart; holds lapel canvas, interfacings and underlinings flat, never crosses the roll line), slip basting (match a pattern: fold one edge on its seam line and stitch it onto the other piece from the right side).
- **Sewing order**: 1. thread-trace; 2. baste seam with right sides together or slip-baste from the right side for checked or striped cloth; 3. fit; 4. stitch beside the basting, not through it; 5. remove the basting before pressing so that the thread does not leave an imprint.
- **Finishes and notions**: soft cotton basting thread (Dual Duty basting cotton or silk thread; it breaks easily and leaves no mark); fine needle (sharps 9 to 10 or milliner's); beeswax is not used on basting thread.
- **Fabrics**: essential on pile, velvet, stripes, plaids, slippery silks, chiffon, and for setting sleeves. Press the basted seam lightly or not at all; a wool press with basting still in can print the thread.
- **Fit faults**: fabric shifts while stitching -> baste closer (every 1 cm) or add a row of diagonal basting; plaids do not match -> slip-baste from the right side with the seam line notched in the pattern.
- **Variants**: replaces pins on leather alternatives (see #leather-lambskin-cowhide); thread tracing is the marking step; related to #catch-stitch.
- **CLO3D**: no equivalent. Model with sewn lines; use pins (Pin tool) only to hold a position during simulation, then remove (a pin is not a stitch).
- **Confidence**: trade practice; stitch lengths from memory (Shaeffer, Couture Sewing Techniques; Reader's Digest Complete Guide to Sewing).

## Underlining (mounting, backing) {#underlining}

- **Aliases**: backing, mounting, flatlining; fr: doublure de montage, doublage, montage
- **Family**: couture techniques, body and support
- **Recognize**: seams inside look like a single layer of the fashion fabric; a second fabric (silk organza, cotton batiste, lawn, silk crepe) is sewn to the wrong side of every piece before assembly; sheer fabric garments with opaque body; darts sewn through both layers
- **Pieces**: one underlining piece per shell piece (same count), cut from the same pattern with the same grain and the same SA; facings usually not underlined (interface or leave single); hem facing strip of underlining if weight is needed
- **Shape**: Underlining is identical to the shell outline. It does not change the net pattern. Underlining is cut with the same SA and the same grain line, and it is basted to the wrong side of the shell, 1 to 1.5 cm in from the cut edge and along the net lines of darts and fold lines, before any seam is stitched. Darts: stitched through both layers; the underlining and shell are handled as one layer afterwards. Source: unverified (trade practice).
- **Drafting**:
  1. Cut underlining from the full pattern of every piece that needs support; for slightly more stability, cut the underlining 2 to 3 mm smaller than the shell at the SA edge (some tailors) or cut identical (most common).
  2. Match the straight grain exactly; lay the underlining first, then the shell right side up, and baste in the centre and along the SA (even basting 8 to 12 mm, then diagonal basting across large pieces).
  3. Transfer darts and pockets by thread tracing through both layers.
- **Sewing order**: 1. mark; 2. baste underlining to each shell piece; 3. stitch darts through both layers; 4. assemble as one layer; 5. press the SA open and cat-stitch or trim the underlining to 1 cm so it does not show through.
- **Finishes and notions**: silk organza for crisp structure (#silk-organza), cotton batiste or lawn for body on light wool, silk crepe de chine for warmth on chiffon, flannel or fleece for warmth (interlining, not underlining); the underlining fibre should be comparable to the shell in care and drape.
- **Fabrics**: brocade, satin, silk shantung, wool crepe and lace over a lining. The underlining reduces wrinkling, takes strain off loose weaves and stops seams from showing through. It adds weight and reduces drape: choose a weight lighter than the shell.
- **Fit faults**: shell pulls or puckers at darts -> underlining cut with grain off; garment too stiff -> use a softer underlining or underline only the bodice, not the skirt.
- **Variants**: #silk-organza (the most used underlining); flatlining is the term for a fabric used as a backing by the home sewer; interlining (insulation) is between shell and lining (see ST section 7); interfacing (#interfacing-fusible-woven) stays local.
- **CLO3D**: not modelled as a separate sewn layer unless it matters; either raise the fabric weight and thickness to include the underlining or add a second pattern layer on the same outline, with the underlining pattern at the same position (offset by the thickness in the layer order), and merge the physical properties in the fabric.
- **Confidence**: trade practice (Shaeffer, Couture Sewing Techniques; Reader's Digest). Same-size cutting rule widely stated; the 2-3 mm smaller variant is from memory.

## Silk organza (as a couture sewing aid) {#silk-organza}

- **Aliases**: organza underlining, organza stay, organza hem facing, organza interfacing; fr: organza de soie, entoilage organza
- **Family**: couture techniques, support materials
- **Recognize**: crisp, transparent, strong, thin plain-weave silk (typically 14 to 16 g/m2 per layer; it varies); visible when a hem is lifted or a bodice is turned out: a stiffened, fine layer inside a seam, facing or hem
- **Pieces**: bias or straight-grain strips 2 to 3 cm wide (stays), full pieces (underlining of bodice pieces), hem facing (strips 5 to 10 cm wide following the hem curve), facings and collar interfacing (pattern minus 3 mm at the SA edges)
- **Shape**: As a stay, a straight-grain strip is cut to the exact length of the seam line (not the cut edge) and caught in the seam. This fixes the edge at its net length and stops stretch on the neckline, shoulder, armhole, waist seam and a zip opening. As a hem facing the strip follows the hem outline (a curve if the hem is curved): cut it on the bias for a flared hem so it conforms; cut it straight for a straight hem. As an interfacing, the pattern of the piece is used with 3 mm removed at the cut edge and at the fold. Source: unverified (trade practice).
- **Drafting**:
  1. Stay: strip 1.5 to 2.5 cm wide, straight grain, length = the net seam line (measured on the pattern). Stitch it into the seam, 1 to 2 mm inside the line, on the seam side that must stay stable.
  2. Underlining: cut each piece from the shell pattern; baste; see #underlining.
  3. Hem facing: strip width = hem depth + 1.5 cm; place it so that it ends 1 cm above the hem fold; catch-stitch (#catch-stitch) the facing and the hem.
- **Sewing order**: 1. cut organza on grain; 2. baste to the wrong side of the shell; 3. stitch seams through shell and organza; 4. press; 5. finish the edge of the organza with a narrow overlock or catch stitch if loose.
- **Finishes and notions**: use a sharp needle (60/8 to 70/10), small stitch (2 mm), wash and press the organza first if the shell is washable; the organza frays; trim it to 1 cm in the allowances.
- **Fabrics**: cotton organdie or polyester organza can replace silk organza for a washable garment; silk organza is chosen for pressing at high heat and for breathability, and because it takes dye well. Do not use stiff polyester organza under chiffon (rubbing).
- **Fit faults**: seam stretches -> add stay strip; bodice wrinkles -> underline with organza; hem shows an edge ridge -> pink or feather the organza edge, or catch-stitch only the top.
- **Variants**: see #underlining, #hem-couture, #interfacing-canvas-horsehair (organza is also used as a sew-in interfacing for blouse collars).
- **CLO3D**: model as a second layer of thin fabric under the shell with its own physical properties (thin, low stretch), or ignore and raise the shell stiffness; do not model stay strips unless the strip changes the pattern outline.
- **Confidence**: trade practice. Weight range and strip widths from memory (Shaeffer; Susan Khalje, Threads articles).

## Catch stitch {#catch-stitch}

- **Aliases**: cross stitch hem, herringbone stitch, flat catch stitch, blind catch stitch, tailor's catch stitch; fr: point d'épine, point de chausson, point de croix-épine
- **Family**: hand stitches
- **Recognize**: a row of small crossed stitches visible on the wrong side only as pairs of X-shaped marks; used to hold a hem edge, interfacing edge or canvas edge flat; nothing visible on the right side
- **Pieces**: no extra piece; the SA edge or hem edge must be flat and free (pinked, finished with seam binding, or raw if the fabric does not fray)
- **Shape**: the stitch holds two layers with a slight give. It does not change the net outline; it needs a hem or edge width of 2.5 to 4 cm (straight hems) so the stitching sits about 2 mm below the top of the edge. Source: unverified (trade practice).
- **Drafting**:
  1. Mark the hem fold line.
  2. Fold the hem allowance up and press. Pink the raw edge or bind it (see ST section 2).
  3. Work left to right, with the needle pointing left: take a tiny stitch (2 to 3 mm) in the garment just above the hem edge, then a tiny stitch in the hem layer 6 to 12 mm to the right; the thread crosses between the stitches in the shape of an X (stitch length 6 to 12 mm; typical).
- **Sewing order**: used after the hem is pressed, before the lining is attached.
- **Finishes and notions**: silk or polyester hand-sewing thread, matched to the shell; do not pull the thread tight (the stitch gives slightly when the fabric moves).
- **Fabrics**: wool, wool crepe, heavy cotton, canvas edges in tailoring, interfacing edges (hair canvas edge to shell), fur lining attachments.
- **Fit faults**: ridge at the hem -> stitch looser and keep the stitch above the hem edge; hem shows from the right -> use a shorter bite in the shell (1 to 2 threads).
- **Variants**: blind stitch machine (#machine-blind-hem) is the industrial counterpart; #hem-couture; #pad-stitching is a similar stitch but with a different job (shaping).
- **CLO3D**: not modelled; make the hem as a folded edge.
- **Confidence**: trade practice (stitch is in all standard stitch dictionaries; hem-edge widths from memory).

## Hand-picked zip {#hand-picked-zip}

- **Aliases**: pick stitch zip, prick stitch zip, hand-stitched zipper, couture zip; fr: fermeture à glissière piquée à la main, point arrière piqué
- **Family**: couture techniques, closures
- **Recognize**: a zip in a seam with tiny dot-like stitches visible on the right side, about 2 to 3 mm from the seam, spaced 3 to 6 mm apart; seam appears soft and flat without a machine topstitching line
- **Pieces**: zip (concealed or standard), seam allowance at least 2 cm on the zip seam, underlap strip, optional stay tape of silk organza along the seam (#silk-organza)
- **Shape**: the pattern needs a 2 cm SA at the zip opening (1.5 cm is workable); the zip seam is the same outline as the non-zip seam, but the seam is basted closed from the zip stop (notch) to the top. For a lapped application, the left side's SA is folded back and the right side's SA is turned in to form a 6 mm to 1 cm lap. Source: unverified (trade practice).
- **Drafting**:
  1. Add a notch at the zip stop (end of the opening).
  2. Add 2 cm SA on both sides of the opening; interface the edge if the cloth is soft.
  3. Zip length = opening length + 1 to 2 cm (the zip stop sits at the notch).
- **Sewing order**: 1. stitch the seam closed below the zip stop and baste closed above it; 2. press the seam open; 3. place the closed zip face down on the SA with the teeth on the seam line and baste each tape to the SA only (not to the shell); 4. from the right side, work a prick stitch (small backstitch that shows as a dot on the surface and 6 mm long underneath) through shell and the zip tape, 6 to 10 mm from the seam, spaced 4 to 6 mm; 5. remove the basting.
- **Finishes and notions**: heavy silk thread or buttonhole twist, waxed; a standard (non-invisible) zip or a hand-sewn invisible zip; hook and bar above the zip.
- **Fabrics**: silk, satin, wool crepe, heavy cotton; important on pile where machine foot pressure leaves marks.
- **Fit faults**: zip puckers -> stitches too tight or tape not relaxed; seam closes unevenly -> notch the SA edge and match the stripes before basting.
- **Variants**: see ST section 5.1 for lapped and centred machine zips; concealed zip with a machine foot is the industrial alternative.
- **CLO3D**: use the zipper trim in the GUI; the API does not support zip seams (`set_zipper_style` exists but placement is a hand step); the stitch is not modelled.
- **Confidence**: trade practice; 2 cm SA from ST and Itch to Stitch notes in ST section 12; stitch spacing from memory.

## Couture hem {#hem-couture}

- **Aliases**: hand-sewn hem, blind hem by hand, hem with horsehair braid, weighted hem; fr: ourlet main, ourlet invisible à la main, ourlet de haute couture
- **Family**: couture techniques, hems
- **Recognize**: no visible stitching on the right side; hem edge is flat with no ridge; interior edge is sewn with tiny stitches; heavy skirts may have a hem tape, a bias strip of interfacing or a chain weight
- **Pieces**: hem allowance (3.5 to 5 cm on a straight skirt; 1 to 1.5 cm for a full circle skirt; 4 cm on a coat; typical values), hem facing (bias strip when the hem curves), optional bias interfacing 4 to 6 cm wide, horsehair braid (hem of full skirts), hem weight or chain
- **Shape**: straight hem allowance is cut parallel to the hem line. On a flared skirt the hem line is a longer curve than the line 4 cm above it, so the hem allowance edge is longer than the finished hem fold: eased by 1.5 to 2.5 cm per 50 cm around the hem (typical, varies with the flare), or a separate bias facing of the same width is applied. For circle and bias skirts, the hem allowance is cut narrow (1 to 1.5 cm) and rolled; hang the skirt for at least 24 hours before marking the hem (see ST section 12 references). Source: unverified (trade practice).
- **Drafting**:
  1. Hem allowance = 4 cm for a straight or slightly flared skirt; reduce to 1 to 2 cm as the flare increases.
  2. For a flared skirt use a bias facing: cut a strip on the bias of width = hem allowance + 1.5 cm and length = the hem circumference; stitch it to the hem edge with 6 mm SA, turn it up, press, and slip-stitch the top.
  3. Interfacing: cut a bias strip of hair canvas or lightweight interfacing 4 to 6 cm wide; catch-stitch it to the hem fold line, then sew the hem allowance to it.
- **Sewing order**: 1. hang; 2. mark the hem line; 3. trim the allowance to even width; 4. finish the edge (bind, pink, overlock); 5. press the fold; 6. attach interfacing and catch-stitch or blind stitch; 7. press without ridging; 8. attach a hem weight inside at the seams if needed.
- **Finishes and notions**: seam binding (Hug Snug, bias cut rayon), horsehair braid (2 to 15 cm; sewn flat to the hem edge then turned up), lead weights or chain weights (in the side seams and CF, hem allowance side).
- **Fabrics**: wool and suiting: 3.5 to 5 cm and hair canvas; silk: 1 to 2 cm rolled hem or hand-rolled hem; chiffon: hand-rolled or narrow machine-rolled hem 3 mm; velvet: bound edge with seam binding, press with a needleboard.
- **Fit faults**: hem hangs unevenly -> bias grain stretched; mark again after hanging; ridge shows -> use a bias interfacing and press from the wrong side with a clapper; flared skirt hem cups -> shrink the hem edge with an iron.
- **Variants**: see ST section 2 for the machine hems; #chiffon-georgette-sewing for sheer fabrics; #catch-stitch is the hem stitch.
- **CLO3D**: set the hem as a folded edge with an allowance if the GUI allows; otherwise end the pattern at the hem line (CLO3D adds no allowance) and increase the hem stiffness by adding a trim or a narrow strip of a stiffer fabric.
- **Confidence**: trade practice. Hanging time verified in ST section 12 (Threads, Janome); allowance widths from memory.

## Pad stitching {#pad-stitching}

- **Aliases**: padding stitch, pad stitch, hand padding, machine pad stitch; fr: point de bourdon d'entoilage, piqûre d'entoilage, points de piqûre à la main
- **Family**: tailoring techniques, shaping
- **Recognize**: on the wrong side of an open lapel or under collar, rows of small slanted stitches arranged in parallel lines through the canvas and the shell, giving the lapel or collar a permanent roll; right side shows no stitches
- **Pieces**: front canvas (lapel part), chest piece (#canvas-fronts), under collar (#under-collar-shaping), lapel facing is not pad stitched; canvas bias strip for the under collar when canvas is used
- **Shape**: pad stitching joins canvas to shell with a stitch that shortens the canvas relative to the shell on the roll side. The lapel canvas is cut at the roll line and rolls over the stitching rows; the lapel facing is cut slightly larger than the lapel at the outer edge so it can turn (the "turn of cloth", 3 to 6 mm over the lapel length; typical). No change to the net outline, but the pattern carries a roll line (from the BP to the neck-side end of the lapel), a stay tape position and the canvas edge. Source: unverified (trade practice).
- **Drafting**:
  1. Mark the roll line on the canvas (BP to a point 1.5 to 2.5 cm from the NP, typical; see #suit-jacket-single-breasted).
  2. Pad stitch rows 1 cm apart (6 mm near the roll line, 1.2 cm further away; typical), stitch length 6 to 8 mm in the canvas and a very small stitch in the shell (1 to 2 threads), thread silk or fine polyester, rows run parallel to the roll line on the lapel (direction differs between authors).
  3. Roll the lapel on the left hand while stitching so that the canvas is slightly shorter than the shell on the face side.
- **Sewing order**: 1. cut the canvas; 2. baste canvas to the front (diagonal basting, #hand-basting); 3. tape the roll line (#roll-line-taping); 4. pad stitch the lapel; 5. shrink the facing; 6. sew the facing.
- **Finishes and notions**: hair canvas (#interfacing-canvas-horsehair), silk thread, thimble, beeswax; a machine pad stitcher is used in industry.
- **Fabrics**: wool, woollen flannel, tweed, camel hair; not for slippery silks.
- **Fit faults**: lapel does not roll -> rows too far apart or stitched flat; lapel rolls too high -> roll line taped too high or too tight; lapel edge curls -> stitching too tight at the edge.
- **Variants**: fusible-canvas jackets skip pad stitching; machine pad stitching in industry; #catch-stitch holds canvas edges.
- **CLO3D**: not modelled; set the fold line and bending stiffness on the lapel through a thicker, stiffer fabric piece or a pre-bent fold angle in the 3D arrangement; roll must be placed by hand.
- **Confidence**: trade practice (Cabrera and Meyers; Shaeffer). Row spacing from memory; direction of rows varies by source.

## Taping the roll line {#roll-line-taping}

- **Aliases**: roll line tape, lapel tape, stay tape on the lapel; fr: ruban de la ligne de cassure, fil de tailleur, pose du ruban de revers
- **Family**: tailoring techniques, shaping
- **Recognize**: a narrow tape (1 cm) sewn on the inside of the lapel along the line where it folds; the lapel breaks softly above the first button
- **Pieces**: tape (twill tape or linen tape or fusible tape, 1 cm wide), length = roll line length minus shortening; roll line is marked on the pattern
- **Shape**: The roll line goes from BP at the front edge to the neckline at the shoulder end (typically a point 1.5 to 2.5 cm from NP towards the shoulder, depending on the collar); the tape is placed just inside the roll line on the body side (about 2 to 3 mm), so that the line folds on the tape edge. The tape is shortened 3 to 10 mm (typical) with the iron or by easing so that the lapel clings to the chest. Source: unverified (trade practice).
- **Drafting**:
  1. Mark the roll line on the pattern from BP to the collar break point.
  2. Measure it. Cut the tape 0.6 to 1 cm shorter than the line (varies; stable fabrics 3 to 6 mm).
  3. Place the tape on the canvas, pin from BP to the neck, then ease the canvas at the middle.
- **Sewing order**: 1. canvas basted; 2. tape pinned and stitched with a catch stitch or a long fell stitch to the canvas only; 3. pad stitch the lapel; 4. press with steam over a ham.
- **Finishes and notions**: cotton twill tape, linen tape, or a fusible straight-grain stay tape.
- **Fabrics**: all wools; reduce the shortening in heavy cloth.
- **Fit faults**: lapel gaps off the chest -> tape too long; lapel fold twists -> tape not straight on grain or on the true roll line.
- **Variants**: also used on the front edge of the jacket; see #canvas-fronts and ST section 4.6.
- **CLO3D**: not modelled; the roll line must be placed as a fold line by hand in the 3D window.
- **Confidence**: trade practice (tape width 1 cm and the shortening range from memory).

## Canvas fronts (hair canvas front) {#canvas-fronts}

- **Aliases**: front canvas, floating canvas, hand-canvassed front, canvas and chest piece; fr: entoilage, entoilage à la main, plastron
- **Family**: tailoring techniques, structure
- **Recognize**: a layer of hair canvas between the shell and facing of the front from shoulder to a point above the hem, with a chest piece on top; produces a soft chest, a clean roll and a drape that holds
- **Pieces**: front canvas (2, one per front), chest piece (2, bias cut or hair canvas), bias hem strip, shoulder and armhole stay tape, front edge tape 1 cm, optional wadding (lamb's wool) for the chest; also cuts for sleeve heads (#sleeve-head)
- **Shape**: canvas is cut from the front pattern. The outline follows the front: front edge at the net line (not including the SA), neckline, shoulder at the net line, armhole and side. The bottom stops at the hem fold or 2 to 3 cm above it. The front dart is cut out of the canvas and the edges lapped 3 to 5 mm, stitched with a zigzag, to avoid bulk. The canvas grain is parallel to the front edge, and the lapel zone may be cut so the warp runs along the roll line (varies; verify). The canvas stops 1 cm inside the front edge net line so it does not enter the seam. The chest piece is a curve from the shoulder to the armhole, about 15 to 20 cm long and 10 to 15 cm deep (typical, adult size), on top of the canvas at the chest. Source: unverified (trade practice).
- **Drafting**:
  1. Trace the front pattern at the net line.
  2. Cut the dart to the pattern: slash the dart on the canvas; overlap by the dart width minus 3 mm; stitch.
  3. Remove the SA at the front edge and hem; keep the SA at the shoulder and armhole.
  4. Chest piece: draw a curve from the neck 2 cm from NP across to the armhole, about 15 to 20 cm wide and 10 to 15 cm deep (not a fixed number; verify on the toile).
- **Sewing order**: 1. interface canvas edges with tape; 2. baste the canvas to the front; 3. pad stitch the lapel (#pad-stitching); 4. stitch the chest piece; 5. attach the facing and press.
- **Finishes and notions**: hair canvas (cotton warp, hair or wool weft), wool or cotton wadding, linen tape.
- **Fabrics**: all tailored wool; for lightweight fabrics, use fusible weft-insertion (#interfacing-weft-insertion).
- **Fit faults**: chest hollow -> chest piece too small or not shaped; lapel gapes -> roll tape too long; hem ridge -> canvas ends too close to the hem, or no bias strip.
- **Variants**: fusible fronts (half-canvas); see ST section 4.6 and #interfacing-canvas-horsehair.
- **CLO3D**: not available as a canvas; either model the front as a stiffer, thicker fabric piece or add a second layer of canvas fabric on the front pattern.
- **Confidence**: trade practice. Dart handling and canvas outline from memory (Cabrera and Meyers); chest piece dimensions are typical, not verified.

## Under collar shaping {#under-collar-shaping}

- **Aliases**: undercollar, bias under collar, collar roll shaping, collar stand and fall shaping; fr: col dessous, travail du col tailleur
- **Family**: tailoring techniques, shaping
- **Recognize**: on a tailored jacket the collar stands up against the neck then rolls over cleanly; the under collar is a separate piece (often melton or felt, in a different fabric from the top collar) with a seam at CB when cut on the bias
- **Pieces**: under collar (1 piece cut on the straight grain, or 2 pieces cut on the bias with a CB seam pressed open), top collar (1, cut larger than the under collar), canvas or collar interfacing (1 or 2), stay tape along the neck edge
- **Shape**: The under collar is a curved band. Its neck edge (sewn to the garment neckline from the gorge point to CB) is concave and shorter; its outer edge is convex and longer; the roll line lies between them and is curved concave towards the neck (the more curved the roll line, the more the collar stands). The neck edge is drafted to the garment neckline length measured at the net line. The top collar is cut 0.6 to 1.2 cm wider and 0.6 to 1.0 cm longer at the outer edge than the under collar (typical turn of cloth; verify on the toile) so the seam rolls to the underside. The curve is then fixed by heat: stretch the outer edge (fall) and shrink the neck edge (stand) on a ham (#shrink-stretch-iron). Source: unverified (trade practice); the collar stand and fall dimensions come from the collar drafts in `../techniques/sleeves-collars.md` and #suit-jacket-single-breasted.
- **Drafting**:
  1. Draft the collar on the measured neckline; stand typical 2.5 to 3.5 cm, fall typical 3.5 to 4.5 cm; curve the neck edge and the roll line (see sleeves-collars.md).
  2. Cut the under collar on the bias (45 degrees) in two parts with a CB seam: the bias grain lets the piece stretch and curve; press the CB seam open.
  3. Top collar: straight grain along CB; add 0.6 to 1.0 cm to the outer edge length and width.
- **Sewing order**: 1. stitch the CB seam of the under collar; 2. pad stitch the canvas to the under collar (#pad-stitching); 3. shape with steam; 4. attach the neck edge to the garment; 5. attach the top collar to the facing and join them.
- **Finishes and notions**: hair canvas or collar canvas, melton for the under collar of wool jackets, silk thread.
- **Fabrics**: wool and wool blends; on fine silk and cotton use a fusible interfacing (#interfacing-fusible-woven).
- **Fit faults**: collar stands away from the neck -> neck edge too long or roll line not curved enough; collar sits too low -> stand too small or collar not shaped; collar edge curls up -> top collar not large enough.
- **Variants**: shirt collar and stand (see sleeves-collars.md); #pad-stitching.
- **CLO3D**: model as a collar piece and a stand piece (or one piece with a fold line at the roll line) in a stiff fabric; the curve exists only through the pattern; heat shaping is not modelled.
- **Confidence**: trade practice. Dimensions typical; the shrink and stretch directions are standard, exact amounts not known.

## Setting sleeves with sleeve heads {#sleeve-head}

- **Aliases**: sleeve head, sleeve roll, sleeve cap support, cap wadding, lambswool header; fr: bourrelet de manche, ouate de manche
- **Family**: tailoring techniques, sleeves
- **Recognize**: the top of a jacket sleeve rises above the shoulder seam with a soft roll; the cap is smooth with no puckers; a strip of padding is visible when the armhole seam is opened
- **Pieces**: sleeve head strip (2, one per sleeve), shoulder pad (2), sleeve cap ease (a property of the sleeve pattern), armhole stay tape
- **Shape**: The sleeve cap is drafted longer than the armhole; the difference is the cap ease. Typical cap ease for tailoring in wool is 2.5 to 5 cm (varies with fabric; woven cotton 2 to 3, stable synthetics less). The ease is spread between the cap notches (leaving the underarm area flat) and is concentrated around the shoulder dot. The sleeve head is a strip of lambswool, wadding or felt cut on the bias, about 20 to 25 cm long and 3 to 5 cm wide (typical, adult), centred on the shoulder dot with its ends near the cap notches, stitched into the seam allowance on the sleeve side. Source: unverified (trade practice; sizes typical).
- **Drafting**:
  1. Measure the sleeve cap and the armhole along the net lines; cap ease = the difference.
  2. Mark the ease limits with notches and the shoulder dot.
  3. Cut the sleeve head on the bias.
- **Sewing order**: 1. ease the cap with two rows of gathering stitch (3.5 to 4 mm) between the notches; 2. shrink the ease with steam over a sleeve board or ham until smooth; 3. baste the sleeve into the armhole from the sleeve side; 4. machine stitch with the sleeve on top; 5. stitch the sleeve head to the seam allowance with the folded edge at the seam; 6. attach the shoulder pad.
- **Finishes and notions**: bias strip of lambswool, wadding, felt or hair canvas, or a ready-made sleeve roll; shoulder pad.
- **Fabrics**: wool and tweed take ease easily; silk, crepe and synthetics shrink less, so reduce the cap ease.
- **Fit faults**: puckers at the cap -> too much ease or not shrunk; cap collapses -> no sleeve head or too thin; drag lines from the cap to the underarm -> cap too low or too narrow (see sleeves-collars.md).
- **Variants**: shirt sleeve (flat cap, 0 to 2 cm ease, no sleeve head); #shrink-stretch-iron.
- **CLO3D**: sew the cap to the armhole with unequal lengths and let the sewing ratio ease it; a cap ease above 5 cm tends to collapse in the simulation; the sleeve head and shoulder pad are not available and must be built by hand (narrow strip pattern or locally thicker fabric).
- **Confidence**: trade practice. Ease ranges from memory (Cabrera and Meyers; Armstrong); strip dimensions typical.

## Shrinking and stretching with the iron {#shrink-stretch-iron}

- **Aliases**: steam shaping, heat shaping, easing, blocking; fr: crochetage au fer, rétrécir et allonger à la vapeur
- **Family**: tailoring techniques, pressing
- **Recognize**: curved edges lie flat after pressing; a collar roll, sleeve cap or trouser knee holds its shape without darts or extra seams
- **Pieces**: no extra piece; the pattern carries notches and a length difference between mating edges
- **Shape**: Shrinking reduces the length of an eased edge by about 1 to 2 percent on wool and wool blends (less on synthetics and silk, little on cotton and linen) with steam; stretching lengthens a bias or curved edge by about 1 to 3 percent with steam and pull (typical, from memory). In the pattern this is a length difference: the long edge is longer than the short edge and both are notched at the ends; the iron removes the difference. Examples: sleeve cap ease (#sleeve-head); jacket back shoulder longer than the front shoulder (1.0 to 1.5 cm, see #blazer-womens); flared hem edge; under collar (#under-collar-shaping). Source: unverified (trade practice).
- **Drafting**:
  1. Add notches at the start and end of the length difference.
  2. Do not draft more difference than the fabric can lose: wool 1 to 2 percent of the edge, silk and synthetics under 1 percent.
  3. Test the amount on a scrap with steam and a clapper.
- **Sewing order**: stitch a gathering row 3 mm from the net line on the long edge, pull it to the length of the short edge, set with steam and a damp press cloth, press until dry, and leave on the ham or form until cold.
- **Finishes and notions**: tailor's ham, sleeve board, clapper, press cloth, wool pad, steam iron (148 to 160 C for wool, ST section 7).
- **Fabrics**: works on wool, wool blends, hair canvas and linen; polyester and nylon barely shrink; velvet, leather and faux leather cannot be steamed (#velvet-pile, #leather-lambskin-cowhide).
- **Fit faults**: ease puckers -> too much ease or not shrunk; piece loses its shape -> moved before it cooled; shine -> press cloth too thin or iron too hot.
- **Variants**: stay tape (#roll-line-taping) stops stretching.
- **CLO3D**: not modelled directly; use sewing length ratios and the shrinkage values of the fabric (`set_shrinkage`).
- **Confidence**: trade practice. Percentages from memory (Cabrera and Meyers; Shaeffer), not verified online.

- **Variants**: #machine-post-bed, free-arm home machines.
- **CLO3D**: not relevant.
- **Confidence**: trade practice.

- **Family**: machine accessories
- **Recognize**: a needle is described by its system, point and size (metric NM, e.g. 70 to 110; US 10 to 18)
- **Pieces**: no special piece
- **Shape**: sizes: 60 to 70 for chiffon, organza and lawn; 70 to 80 for silk, light cotton and fine knits; 80 to 90 for wool suiting, medium cotton and jersey; 90 to 100 for denim and canvas; 100 to 110 or more for heavy denim, upholstery and leather (ST section 9 lists 70/10 to 100/16). - **Fabrics**: match the needle tip to the fibre: sharp for silk, rounded for knits, cutting for leather.

## Silk and satin: seams and hems {#silk-satin-sewing}

- **Aliases**: silk crepe de chine, charmeuse, duchess satin, habotai, silk taffeta, polyester satin; fr: soie, satin, crêpe de Chine, charmeuse
- **Family**: fabric-specific construction
- **Recognize**: smooth, slippery, fluid fabric with sheen (satin has long floats on the face); needle holes and water spots show; seams slip at stress
- **Pieces**: no special piece. Add underlining or stay strips (#silk-organza) at stressed seams; facings narrow; interfacing with silk organza or light weft-insertion
- **Shape**: SA is 1.5 cm for plain or French seams (a narrower SA slips out); French seam needs 1.5 cm (ST section 1.1); hairline seam 1 cm; hem allowance 1 to 2.5 cm (narrow double-fold) or a rolled hem 3 mm. Satin is cut one-way, in a single layer, with the same direction for all pieces (the sheen changes with direction; the cut edge of a bias skirt needs a 24 hour hang, ST section 12). Source: unverified (trade practice; ranges from ST).
- **Drafting**:
  1. Cut in a single layer, grain-matched, with sharp shears or a rotary cutter and weights instead of pins.
  2. Add 1.5 cm SA; avoid seams on curves that need clipping.
  3. Add a 6 mm stay tape or a silk organza strip at necklines, shoulder and waist.
- **Sewing order**: stay-stitch curves, baste the seams, sew with a very small stitch (2 to 2.5 mm, ST section 0), press with a cloth.
- **Finishes and notions**: sharp or microtex needle 60/8 to 70/10 (#needles-by-fabric), fine polyester or silk thread, a straight-stitch plate, tissue paper under the seam if it puckers; French, hairline or bound seams; hems narrow double-fold or hand-rolled (#hem-couture).
- **Fabrics**: charmeuse and satin snag and mark; duchess satin is stiffer and can hold shape; silk taffeta creases permanently; do not unpick (leaves holes).
- **Fit faults**: seam puckers -> finer needle, shorter stitch, reduce tension; seam slips -> wider SA, underline; water marks -> press dry with a cloth, test for steam.
- **Variants**: #chiffon-georgette-sewing for sheers.
- **CLO3D**: set low stiffness, low thickness, high drape; satin has a smooth specular material; no grain direction issue except bias-cut pieces.
- **Confidence**: trade practice (ST section 1.1, 7 and Shaeffer).

## Chiffon and georgette: seams and hems {#chiffon-georgette-sewing}

- **Aliases**: chiffon, georgette, crepe georgette, voile, organza when crisp; fr: mousseline, georgette
- **Family**: fabric-specific construction
- **Recognize**: sheer, light, often slightly crinkled (georgette uses twisted crepe yarns, giving a matte crinkle and more body); edges fray and slide; seams must look neat from the right side because they are visible through the fabric
- **Pieces**: no special piece. Usually lined or underlined; narrow facings or bound edges; stay tape on shoulder and neckline
- **Shape**: seams are French or hairline (1.5 cm for French, 1 cm for hairline, ST section 1.1); hem allowances are narrow: rolled hem 3 mm, double-fold 1 to 1.5 cm; a hem on a circle or bias skirt 1 to 1.5 cm and hung 24 hours first (ST section 12). Pieces are cut in a single layer; the pattern can have wider flare because the fabric drapes; darts are replaced by pleats, gathers or ease. Source: unverified.
- **Drafting**:
  1. Cut on tissue paper or a cutting board with weights; the pattern lines carry notches only (small snips) not deep notches.
  2. Add 1.5 cm SA at French seams and 1 cm at hairline seams; no clipping into SA beyond 2 mm.
  3. Hems: add 1 to 1.5 cm for a rolled or double-fold hem.
- **Sewing order**: stay-stitch edges, sew French or hairline seams, press seams to one side, finish the hem last after hanging.
- **Finishes and notions**: needle 60/8 sharp or microtex, thread fine polyester or silk, stitch 1.5 to 2 mm, tissue paper under the seams if they pucker; rolled-hem foot or the serger rolled hem; weights for the hem of floating skirts.
- **Fabrics**: georgette has more body and grips better; chiffon slips and clings; both attract static.
- **Fit faults**: seam waves -> stitch with tissue paper, shorten the stitch; hem line unevenly hangs -> mark again after hanging.
- **Variants**: #silk-satin-sewing, #hem-couture, #lace-sewing.
- **CLO3D**: low weight (about 30 to 60 g/m2 typical), very low bending stiffness, thin; use a fine mesh (2 to 5 mm particle distance) so that the drape is realistic; transparency set in the material.
- **Confidence**: trade practice; weights typical.

## Velvet and pile fabrics {#velvet-pile}

- **Aliases**: velvet, velveteen, corduroy, plush, panne velvet, stretch velvet; fr: velours, velours côtelé, velours de coton, panne
- **Family**: fabric-specific construction
- **Recognize**: dense pile on one face; colour looks deeper when the pile is brushed up (nap up) and lighter, with more sheen, when it lies down (nap down); marks easily from pressing and from pins
- **Pieces**: no special piece. Interface only with sew-in or fusible knit tape; line the garment
- **Shape**: the pattern must carry a nap arrow: every piece is cut in the same direction (one-way layout), with pile running up or down by choice; a one-way layout uses 10 to 15 percent more fabric (typical). SA 1.5 cm; hem 3 to 4 cm hand-sewn, or a faced hem to avoid stitches that show. Pile pushes in the machine: layers creep, so seams are basted first. Source: unverified (trade practice).
- **Drafting**:
  1. Mark the pile direction on every pattern piece.
  2. Cut a single layer with the pile facing up (face up) or on a double layer with right sides together if the layout is one-way; use sharp shears.
  3. Add the SA that allows for basting (1.5 cm).
- **Sewing order**: baste by hand, sew in the direction of the pile, press from the wrong side on a needleboard or over a scrap of velvet face down with steam only, do not pull.
- **Finishes and notions**: walking foot or roller foot, needle 80/12 (silk velvet 70/10), long stitch 3 mm, hand-picked zip (#hand-picked-zip) or concealed zip; bind or overlock edges; hang the garment on a form to relax.
- **Fabrics**: silk velvet is slippery and fragile; cotton velveteen is heavy; stretch velvet is a knit and needs a ballpoint needle and a stretch stitch.
- **Fit faults**: seam shifts -> baste by hand; pile crushed -> steam and brush; shading between pieces -> not all cut in the same direction.
- **Variants**: corduroy (cut with wales vertical, one-way); velveteen.
- **CLO3D**: set a pile direction by the grain direction of each pattern piece; high thickness; use a rough material with pile or fuzz map for the look.
- **Confidence**: trade practice.

## Denim: seams and hems {#denim-sewing}

- **Aliases**: jeans fabric, selvedge denim, stretch denim, indigo twill; fr: denim, jean, toile de jean
- **Family**: fabric-specific construction
- **Recognize**: cotton twill with indigo warp and white weft; flat-felled seams with double rows of topstitching in contrasting thread (gold), bartacks, rivets, chain-stitched hems
- **Pieces**: no special piece. Pocket bags, waistband facing, yoke, fly; interfacing woven fusible on waistband and fly
- **Shape**: flat-felled seams need 1.5 cm on both pieces, or 2 to 2.5 cm on the fell side (ST section 1.1); side seams usually fell, inseam fell, back rise lap-felled. Hem allowance 2 to 3 cm (jeans chain-stitched) or a cuff. Twill direction: left-hand twill is usual for jeans; cut all pieces with the same twill direction to avoid leg twist. Pre-shrink before cutting: shrinkage 3 to 8 percent in length can occur on raw denim (varies strongly; test). Source: unverified (trade practice; allowance from ST).
- **Drafting**: SA 1.5 cm both sides; 2 cm on the fell side; add a hem allowance of 3 to 4 cm if the hem is double-folded and chainstitched.
- **Sewing order**: back pockets, yoke, inseam, side seam, fly, waistband, hem, bartacks.
- **Finishes and notions**: needle 90/14 to 110/18 jeans (#needles-by-fabric), topstitch thread Tex 70 to 100 (#threads-by-fabric), 4 stitches per cm (3 to 3.5 mm for topstitching), rivets and buttons, bartacks (#machine-bartack), chainstitch for hems (#machine-chainstitch).
- **Fabrics**: selvedge denim has a narrow width (about 80 cm), stretch denim has 1 to 3 percent elastane, lighter chambray denim for shirts; wash before cutting.
- **Fit faults**: twisting legs -> match twill direction and grain; whiskers and fades follow the construction, not the pattern.
- **Variants**: #stitch-401, #stitch-516, `../techniques/skirts-trousers.md` for the jeans draft.
- **CLO3D**: use a stiff, rough twill with high weight (about 300 to 400 g/m2 for 9 to 12 oz denim, typical); topstitch on the seams; set shrinkage by hand.
- **Confidence**: trade practice; shrinkage range from memory.

## Wool suiting: seams and hems {#wool-suiting-sewing}

- **Aliases**: worsted suiting, woollen suiting, flannel, gabardine, tweed, wool crepe; fr: lainage de costume, drap de laine, flanelle, gabardine
- **Family**: fabric-specific construction
- **Recognize**: dense wool twill or plain weave; absorbs steam and takes a press; seams are pressed open; hem edges are bound or covered by lining
- **Pieces**: no special piece. Hair canvas or fusible interfacing, bias hem tape, pocketing, sleeve heads, lining
- **Shape**: SA 1.5 cm (ST section 1.2) pressed open; hem allowance 3.5 to 5 cm for trousers and jackets; a curved hem needs an eased edge or a facing (#hem-couture). Cap ease 2.5 to 5 cm (#sleeve-head). Pattern is cut with the grain, with stripes and checks matched at seams; flannel and tweed may have a nap or twill direction to respect. Pre-shrink by steam or by a cold-water sponge (decatising) before cutting. Source: unverified (trade practice).
- **Drafting**: SA 1.5 cm; add 1 cm extra on seams that may need to be let out (CB, crotch); notches for ease.
- **Sewing order**: interface, press darts, stitch and press each seam, shape with the iron (#shrink-stretch-iron), line and hem.
- **Finishes and notions**: needle 80/12 to 90/14, polyester thread Tex 50, press with a damp cloth at 148 to 160 C (ST section 7), clapper; finish edges with overlock, pinking, or Hong Kong seams.
- **Fabrics**: wool crepe relaxes: hang the garment before hemming; gabardine marks when unpicked; heavy tweed is bulky, so grade the seams.
- **Fit faults**: ripples on the seam -> press over a ham; shine -> press cloth, not too hot.
- **Variants**: #canvas-fronts, #pad-stitching, `../techniques/tailoring-outerwear.md`.
- **CLO3D**: set wool weight (about 250 to 400 g/m2, typical), medium stiffness, low stretch; stripes and checks via a texture map.
- **Confidence**: trade practice.

## Linen: seams and hems {#linen-sewing}

- **Aliases**: flax, linen, linen-cotton blend, handkerchief linen; fr: lin, toile de lin
- **Family**: fabric-specific construction
- **Recognize**: crisp natural fibre that wrinkles, frays and softens with washing; slubs and irregular yarns
- **Pieces**: no special piece. Fusible interfacing sometimes bubbles: use sew-in or a linen-compatible woven
- **Shape**: SA 1.5 cm with a bound, French or flat-felled seam (ST section 1.1) since linen frays; hems 2.5 to 4 cm double-folded or mitred; the cloth relaxes with wear so the fit takes slightly more ease than in cotton. Wash and dry linen two or more times before cutting; it can shrink 3 to 10 percent depending on the weave (typical range, from memory; test). Source: unverified.
- **Drafting**: SA 1.5 cm; add 1 cm in length for shrinkage if not pre-washed.
- **Sewing order**: stay-stitch edges, finish seams, press with a hot moist iron (about 200 C, ST section 7).
- **Finishes and notions**: needle 80/12 to 90/14, cotton or polyester thread, all seams finished; self-binding for facings; buttons of shell or wood.
- **Fabrics**: linen-cotton blends wrinkle less; lightweight handkerchief linen is semi-sheer.
- **Fit faults**: seam fraying -> finish edges; grain pulls out of shape -> cut on grain, not off-grain.
- **Variants**: #silk-satin-sewing (opposite behaviour); cotton.
- **CLO3D**: medium weight (about 130 to 250 g/m2), high bending stiffness relative to cotton, texture map with slubs.
- **Confidence**: trade practice; shrinkage range from memory.

## Leather: lambskin and cowhide {#leather-lambskin-cowhide}

- **Aliases**: garment leather, nappa, lambskin, cowhide, veg-tan leather, chrome-tanned leather; fr: cuir, agneau, vachette, cuir nappa
- **Family**: fabric-specific construction (leather)
- **Recognize**: whole skins with irregular outlines, natural grain, scars and thickness variation; seams topstitched, glued, hammered flat; edges raw, painted or turned; pins leave permanent holes
- **Pieces**: no special piece. Interfacing or stay tape on stretchy lambskin; leather facings or fabric facings; skived edges; leather binding
- **Shape**: SA 1 cm (1.5 cm on collars, edges; ST section 1.5). Hems may have no allowance (0, raw or burnished edge) or 1.5 to 2.5 cm turned and glued. Cowhide is thicker (about 1.0 to 2.0 mm) and needs **skiving**: the SA is thinned to about half the thickness over the last 5 to 10 mm of the allowance, so folded edges and seams are not bulky. Lambskin is thin (about 0.5 to 0.9 mm), very soft and stretches; it needs stay tape on seams. Pieces are laid out on the hide with the grain line along the backbone (spine), the least stretchy direction; stretchier belly and neck areas go at non-critical places; skins have a limited size, so long pieces need a seam. Thickness values are typical, from memory. Source: unverified.
- **Drafting**:
  1. Pattern pieces are cut single layer and mirrored by flipping the pattern for the second side.
  2. SA 1 cm, notches as small snips of 3 mm; avoid darts that need slashing; use seam shaping and gathers lightly.
  3. Add a lap allowance (6 to 10 mm) at lapped seams; omit the hem allowance for a raw edge.
- **Sewing order**: mark on the wrong side; stay-stitch; glue seam allowances with contact cement and tap flat with a mallet; topstitch with a roller or Teflon foot; edge finish (edge paint, burnishing or binding).
- **Finishes and notions**: no pins (use paper clips, wonder clips, tape), leather (wedge) needle 90/14 to 110/18, thread nylon bonded or polyester Tex 40 to 70, stitch length 3 to 4 mm; **saddle stitch** by hand uses two needles on one thread and a pricking iron, stitch marks 3 to 4 mm, it holds if one stitch breaks (ST section 1.5); edges painted with leather edge paint after sanding.
- **Fabrics**: lambskin for blouses and jackets; cowhide for coats, bags, belts; veg-tan leather is stiff and holds shape; chrome-tanned is soft.
- **Fit faults**: seam puckers -> longer stitch, roller foot; edge bulky -> skive more; leather stretches -> stay tape.
- **Variants**: #suede-nubuck-sewing, #faux-leather-sewing; `../techniques/accessories.md` for bags and belts.
- **CLO3D**: set high bending stiffness, almost no stretch (lambskin a little more), medium thickness; leather grain via normal map; skiving and glue are not modelled.
- **Confidence**: trade practice (ST section 1.5, 2); thickness values from memory.

## Suede and nubuck {#suede-nubuck-sewing}

- **Aliases**: suede, split suede, nubuck, leather with nap; fr: daim, nubuck, croûte de cuir, cuir velours
- **Family**: fabric-specific construction (leather)
- **Recognize**: suede is the flesh side (or a split) of the hide with a short nap; nubuck is the grain side buffed to a very fine nap; both change shade with the direction of the nap; both mark with water and grease
- **Pieces**: no special piece. Light fusible interfacing on the wrong side of areas that must not stretch
- **Shape**: as leather (#leather-lambskin-cowhide): SA 1 cm, hem raw (0) or turned and glued 1.5 to 2.5 cm; the nap direction is a pattern constraint: mark an arrow on every piece and cut all pieces in the same direction. Do not use deep notches; cut notches as small snips. Source: unverified.
- **Drafting**: mark the nap arrow; mark on the wrong (flesh/grain) side with a pencil or chalk; lay patterns on the hide with weights.
- **Sewing order**: glue seam allowances with minimal cement, hammer, topstitch; no steam or iron on the face.
- **Finishes and notions**: no pins; leather needle 80/12 to 100/16; thread polyester or nylon bonded; stitch length 3 mm; roller foot; a brush to raise the nap after sewing.
- **Fabrics**: lightweight suede for blouses and skirts; heavy suede for jackets; nubuck is more delicate.
- **Fit faults**: shading differences -> pieces cut with different nap directions; stains -> treat with a protective spray.
- **Variants**: #leather-lambskin-cowhide.
- **CLO3D**: leather with a nap (fuzz map), no stretch; grain direction arrow set to match the nap.
- **Confidence**: trade practice.

## Faux leather (PU and PVC): seams and hems {#faux-leather-sewing}

- **Aliases**: vegan leather, pleather, leatherette, coated fabric, PU leather; fr: simili-cuir, cuir synthétique, enduit
- **Family**: fabric-specific construction
- **Recognize**: coated fabric (polyurethane or PVC on a woven or knit backing) with an embossed grain; sticks to the presser foot; creases and cracks over time; needle holes remain
- **Pieces**: no special piece. Facings usually in lining fabric; interface the zip seam
- **Shape**: SA 1 to 1.5 cm; no hem allowance is needed on a non-fraying edge (0), or 1.5 to 2.5 cm topstitched; backing with a knit gives 2-way stretch, which sets the ease (non-stretch backing: no stretch, cut as leather). Seams are not pressed open with heat (low iron or finger-pressed). Source: unverified (trade practice; handling rules shared with ST section 1.5).
- **Drafting**: as leather, but with the backing's grain along the main direction; avoid tight curves for thick coatings.
- **Sewing order**: clips instead of pins, roller/Teflon foot, longer stitch 3 to 4 mm, topstitch to flatten seams.
- **Finishes and notions**: needle 80/12 to 100/16 leather or microtex; polyester thread; a strip of tissue paper or tape under the foot; contact cement for hems.
- **Fabrics**: knit-backed PU stretches 20 to 40 percent (verify per roll); PVC is shiny and stiff; microfibre suede-look faux is more forgiving.
- **Fit faults**: sticking -> roller foot; skipped stitches -> new needle, a leather or microtex point; permanent holes -> do not unpick.
- **Variants**: #leather-lambskin-cowhide, #waterproof-technical-fabrics.
- **CLO3D**: stiffer than cloth, low stretch (or knit-like for knit backing), shiny material; the back colour is the backing.
- **Confidence**: trade practice.

## Fur and faux fur {#fur-faux-fur-sewing}

- **Aliases**: faux fur, long-pile fur, fake fur, pelt, shearling; fr: fourrure, fausse fourrure, peau lainée
- **Family**: fabric-specific construction
- **Recognize**: dense pile fibres on a knitted or leather backing; pile direction (the nap) runs from the neck to the tail; the pile hides seams; the fabric is thick and heavy
- **Pieces**: no special piece. Usually fully lined; facings in lining fabric or leather; faux fur is not interfaced, but the hem edge is stabilised with tape
- **Shape**: cut in a single layer, right side down, with the pile running downward (stroking from neck to tail) on every piece; each piece carries a nap arrow. Cut with a razor blade or craft knife through the backing only, not with shears across the pile, so the fibres are not cut. SA 1.5 cm; trim the pile from the SA after sewing. Darts are avoided: replace them by seams or by slash-and-butt (cut dart out, join the edges with zigzag). Pile length changes the pattern: long-pile fur wants simpler shapes, 1 to 3 cm more ease in the body and sleeves than in a woven coat (typical, from memory, verify on the toile). Source: unverified.
- **Drafting**:
  1. Remove facings and pleats; simplify seams and use few pieces.
  2. Add a nap arrow to each piece and chalk the nap on the backing.
  3. Reduce the SA bulk by removing the facings and by trimming pile.
- **Sewing order**: tuck the pile inward while sewing with a pin or needle tip, sew with a long stitch (3.5 to 4 mm), use a walking foot, trim the SA pile, hand-stitch the hem or glove-stitch (whipstitch) seams; line the garment.
- **Finishes and notions**: sturdy needle 90/14 to 100/16, strong polyester thread, hooks and eyes or fur clasps instead of buttonholes, fabric lining; fur hems are hand-finished.
- **Fabrics**: short-pile faux fur is easier; long-pile faux fur has a stretch on the backing in one direction; real fur needs a furrier's machine.
- **Fit faults**: bulky seams -> trim pile; seams show a line -> use a pin to pull the fibres out of the stitching.
- **Variants**: #leather-lambskin-cowhide for shearling (leather side out).
- **CLO3D**: no true fur: use a thick fabric with a fuzz/fur material or a fur trim; the stiffness and thickness must be high; pile direction is a texture direction.
- **Confidence**: trade practice; additional ease is a typical estimate, not verified.

## Neoprene and scuba: seams and hems {#neoprene-sewing}

- **Aliases**: scuba, neoprene fabric, wetsuit fabric, bonded foam knit; fr: néoprène, scuba
- **Family**: fabric-specific construction
- **Recognize**: foam rubber core (closed-cell, 1 to 5 mm) bonded to jersey or nylon on one or both faces; spongy and springy; raw edges are left unfinished on garment scuba; wetsuits have glued and blind-stitched or taped seams
- **Pieces**: no special piece. Taped seams or edge binding for sport items
- **Shape**: **Garment neoprene (scuba knit, 1 to 3 mm)**: SA 1 cm, hems turned 1.5 to 2.5 cm or raw. **Wetsuit neoprene**: butted seams (SA 0) glued with neoprene cement and blind-stitched, then taped inside or out; the pattern is made with negative ease because neoprene stretches 100 to 200 percent in some grades (varies). No darts or heavy gathers. Edges are cut with a rotary cutter, with no allowance for a raw edge. Source: unverified (trade practice; stretch values from memory).
- **Drafting**: garment neoprene: SA 1 cm, ease as for a stable knit (18 to 25 percent stretch, KL section 1); wetsuit: SA 0 (butted) or 6 to 8 mm (taped), strongly negative ease.
- **Sewing order**: clip, sew with a walking foot, press with a low iron through a cloth or finger-press, topstitch to flatten.
- **Finishes and notions**: ballpoint or stretch needle 80/12 to 90/14, polyester thread, zigzag, flatlock or glue and blind stitch; contact cement; seam tape for wetsuits.
- **Fabrics**: scuba knits are thin and stable; wetsuit rubber needs special glues and tape.
- **Fit faults**: seams curl -> edgestitch; surface sticks -> Teflon foot; heat marks -> do not iron hot.
- **Variants**: #stretch-knits-sewing, #stitch-607.
- **CLO3D**: thick, springy fabric with high bending stiffness, stretch as measured; scuba as a ponte-like knit.
- **Confidence**: trade practice; stretch values from memory.

## Waterproof technical fabrics: taping and welding {#waterproof-technical-fabrics}

- **Aliases**: laminated fabric, shell fabric, Gore-Tex type membrane fabric, coated nylon, taped seams, welded seams; fr: tissu technique imperméable, coutures thermocollées, coutures soudées
- **Family**: fabric-specific construction
- **Recognize**: waterproof outerwear (shell jackets, rainwear, tents) with a membrane or coating; seams are covered on the inside with a plastic seam tape (taped seam) or have no stitching at all (welded seam); zips are polyurethane-coated; hoods have visors
- **Pieces**: no special piece. Seam tape (heat-activated, about 16 to 22 mm wide for typical outerwear, from memory), storm flaps, waterproof zip, hood stiffener, welt pockets with welded edges
- **Shape**: **Taped seams**: stitch a standard seam (SA 1 cm), then apply hot-melt seam tape to the inside of the seam, covering the needle holes. The seam path must be taped continuously, so intersections and tight curves are avoided in the pattern (use as few seams as possible, simple shapes, bigger radii). **Welded seams**: pieces are joined by heat, ultrasound or radio frequency with no needle holes; overlap SA is 6 to 12 mm (typical, depends on the machine; not verified); butt welding is used on some TPU laminates. No hem stitching on shell hems that need to be waterproof; a hem may be welded. Source: unverified (trade practice).
- **Drafting**: seam count minimal; mark tape direction and tape ends; add storm flaps and zip garages; avoid darts; use 1 cm SA at seams to be taped (the tape is wider than the SA, covering both sides).
- **Sewing order**: sew, trim, press tape with a taping machine (hot air plus pressure), or weld.
- **Finishes and notions**: fine needle 70/10 to 80/12 to reduce the hole size, polyester or nylon bonded thread, long stitch 3 mm, clips not pins (pin holes leak); no ironing.
- **Fabrics**: 2-layer and 3-layer laminates (face fabric, membrane, liner); PU-coated nylon; waxed cotton (not taped).
- **Fit faults**: leaks at seams -> tape lifted at a curve; tape wrinkles on a curve -> reduce the seam curvature.
- **Variants**: #faux-leather-sewing (coated fabric), outerwear in `../techniques/tailoring-outerwear.md`.
- **CLO3D**: stiff, low stretch, thin; the tape can be modelled as a fine strip or ignored.
- **Confidence**: trade practice; tape width and weld overlap from memory.

## Stretch knits: jersey, rib, interlock and ponte {#stretch-knits-sewing}

- **Aliases**: single jersey, 1x1 rib, 2x2 rib, interlock, ponte di Roma, double knit; fr: jersey, côtes, interlock, ponte, maille
- **Family**: fabric-specific construction (knits)
- **Recognize**: loop-structured fabric that stretches; single jersey curls at the cut edge and has a visible right and wrong side; rib has vertical ridges and high crosswise stretch; interlock is a smooth double jersey that does not curl and is stable; ponte is a heavier, stable double knit with a small stretch
- **Pieces**: no special piece. Neckbands, cuffs and hem bands cut shorter than the opening (KL section 3.1); stay tape on shoulder and neck seams; clear elastic at the waist seam
- **Shape**: SA 1 cm (0.6 cm minimum, ST section 1.2); hem 2 to 2.5 cm; pattern pieces are cut with the stretch crosswise around the body; negative ease depends on the stretch class (KL section 1: stable knit 18 to 25 percent, rib as band 80 to 150 percent, super stretch 76 to 100 percent); band length ratio = 1 / (1 + k x band stretch), k about 0.3 for necklines and 0.4 for cuffs and hems; typical neckline ratios: self jersey 82 to 90 percent, rib 70 to 80 percent, rib with elastane 65 to 75 percent, ponte 95 to 100 percent (KL section 3.1). Source: the stretch classes and ratios are taken from `../techniques/knits-lingerie-swimwear.md` (cited there to Chalk & Notch and Siemacht); the shape of each band is not taken from a drawn pattern.
- **Drafting**: measure the stretch percentage on 10 cm of fabric stretched to its comfortable limit; decide the ease from the stretch class; pattern the band as a rectangle whose length is the opening length times the ratio.
- **Sewing order**: shoulder seams with stay tape, neckband in the round, sleeve set in flat, side seams in one pass, hem last.
- **Finishes and notions**: ballpoint/jersey needle 70/10 to 80/12, 504 or 514 overlock (#stitch-504), coverstitch hems (#stitch-602), stretch stitch or narrow zigzag on a home machine, differential feed 1.0 to 1.5 (ST section 1.4), stable stay tape.
- **Fabrics**: jersey curls: use a walking foot, stabilise the edge; interlock and ponte are stable and take darts; rib needs a ballpoint needle and a stretch thread.
- **Fit faults**: wavy hem -> do not stretch while sewing; neckline gapes -> shorten the band or add stay tape; seams pop -> use a stretch stitch.
- **Variants**: #power-mesh-lycra-sewing, #neoprene-sewing.
- **CLO3D**: set the stretch weft and warp (use the fabric tests), ponte as a heavier, less stretchy knit; apply the negative ease in the pattern, not by scaling.
- **Confidence**: trade practice and verified in KL sheet citations.

## Power mesh and lycra (swim, active, lingerie) {#power-mesh-lycra-sewing}

- **Aliases**: power net, power mesh, swim lycra, nylon-elastane jersey, spandex; fr: résille élastique, tulle power net, lycra
- **Family**: fabric-specific construction (knits)
- **Recognize**: very high stretch (76 to 100 percent, super stretch class, KL section 1), smooth slippery surface (lycra swim), fine open mesh (power mesh) with firm recovery; used for compression panels, linings, swimwear, activewear and lingerie
- **Pieces**: no special piece. Lining pieces in power mesh or swim lining; elastic for edges (fold-over elastic, plush-back elastic); gussets
- **Shape**: strongly negative ease: swim lycra (nylon 80 / elastane 20, stretch 70 to 100 percent in width) uses a body reduction of about 5 to 8 percent at the bust, 10 to 15 percent at the waist and 8 to 12 percent at the hip, with edge elastic at 85 to 95 percent of the edge and leg elastic at about 90 percent (KL section 6.1; sources differ on the absolute value: ASG 2.5 to 7.5 cm full round under the body, other swim sources 8 to 13 cm); power mesh with 100 percent stretch is cut with the stretch around the body (KL section 6.7). Source: KL sheet (cited Chalk & Notch, ASG); piece shapes unverified.
- **Drafting**: pattern in the stretched condition measured at the comfortable limit; decide the stretch from the fabric test; avoid wide darts; use panels.
- **Sewing order**: stabilise seams, attach elastic to the edge, line the crotch and the bust, finish with coverstitch or zigzag.
- **Finishes and notions**: ballpoint/stretch needle 70/10 to 75/11 (microtex on fine mesh), wooly nylon in the loopers, 3-step zigzag or 504/602 stitches; elastic bands; swim lining; clear elastic.
- **Fabrics**: chlorine-resistant elastane for swimwear; power mesh in nylon-elastane 80/20.
- **Fit faults**: swimwear sags -> too little negative ease; seams bite -> wider seam width, flat elastic; elastic puckers -> reduce the ratio.
- **Variants**: #stretch-knits-sewing, `../techniques/knits-lingerie-swimwear.md`.
- **CLO3D**: set high stretch both ways and low weight; mesh as a thin knit, or a transparent texture.
- **Confidence**: trade practice; ratios as stated in KL; ranges differ among sources.

## Lace: seams and hems {#lace-sewing}

- **Aliases**: Chantilly lace, guipure, Alençon lace, corded lace, stretch lace, lace trim; fr: dentelle, guipure
- **Family**: fabric-specific construction
- **Recognize**: openwork fabric with motifs and a scalloped edge; transparent; often with a one-way pattern (motifs have a direction)
- **Pieces**: no special piece. Lining or underlining (#underlining) in a nude or contrast fabric; stay tape at the shoulders and zip; narrow lace or bias binding for edges
- **Shape**: scalloped selvages are used as hem edges, so the hem allowance is 0 and the scallop sits on the finished hem line; motifs are laid out symmetrically at the centre front and back (the layout may need extra fabric); SA 1 to 1.5 cm for plain or French seams; or lapped (appliqué) seams where a motif is cut round and placed over the seam, 0 allowance on the top layer (ST section 1.1, lapped seam). Darts are replaced by seams. Source: unverified.
- **Drafting**: put the scalloped edge at the hem; mirror pieces so the motifs match at CF and CB; mark the lace direction; allow extra fabric for matching the motifs.
- **Sewing order**: underline or line, sew seams (trim the seam allowance to 6 mm and finish), appliqué hand-sewn over the seam to hide it, hem free or with the scallop.
- **Finishes and notions**: fine needle 70/10, thread fine polyester, stitch 2 mm, narrow zigzag for appliqué; French seam on fine lace; lining in nude silk or satin.
- **Fabrics**: stretch lace is a knit; guipure is heavy and open and has no ground, so seams are lapped or zigzagged at the motif edge.
- **Fit faults**: seams show -> hide them with an appliqué over the seam; lace pulls -> underline.
- **Variants**: #chiffon-georgette-sewing, #sequin-beaded-fabric-sewing.
- **CLO3D**: transparent texture on a thin, stretchless fabric; the scallop is a pattern outline; no lapped seam representation.
- **Confidence**: trade practice.

## Sequined and beaded fabrics {#sequin-beaded-fabric-sewing}

- **Aliases**: sequin fabric, beaded fabric, paillettes, sequin mesh, embroidered tulle; fr: tissu à paillettes, tissu perlé, broderie sequins
- **Family**: fabric-specific construction
- **Recognize**: sequins or beads sewn onto a mesh, tulle or knit ground; glittering one-way surface; heavy; the cut edge sheds sequins; stitching hits beads and breaks needles
- **Pieces**: no special piece. Underlining or a lining to protect the skin and carry weight; stay tape in shoulder seams; plain-fabric facings or binding
- **Shape**: pieces are cut one-way (sequins lie in one direction and reflect differently); the seam lines are cleared by removing the sequins or beads from the SA and from the sewing line: allowance 1.5 to 2 cm so that cleared SA does not show. Heavy beading needs hand-sewn seams. Hems are left unhemmed (raw edge with beads removed) or lined; no turn-up. Source: unverified (trade practice).
- **Drafting**: SA 1.5 to 2 cm; mark the direction of the sequins on each piece; reduce seams; avoid darts; line and underline.
- **Sewing order**: cut with old shears; remove the beads or sequins in the SA (pliers or a seam ripper), sew with a large needle (jeans 90/14 or 100/16) and slow speed or by hand; replace the beads over the seam by hand.
- **Finishes and notions**: strong polyester thread; hand-sewn zip or an invisible zip with the sequins cleared; protective lining; steam with caution, never iron the sequins.
- **Fabrics**: sequins on knit ground stretch; on mesh ground they may unravel; plastic sequins melt under an iron.
- **Fit faults**: scratchy interior -> line; heavy hem pulls -> underline.
- **Variants**: #lace-sewing, #underlining.
- **CLO3D**: sequin texture with metallic specular and a bump map; weight is high; layers for the lining.
- **Confidence**: trade practice.

## Felt {#felt-sewing}

- **Aliases**: wool felt, craft felt, pressed felt, acrylic felt, wool-blend felt; fr: feutre, feutrine
- **Family**: fabric-specific construction
- **Recognize**: non-woven fabric made by matting fibres; no grain, no weave, no fraying; thick and stiff for its weight; the cut edge is the finished edge
- **Pieces**: no special piece. No facing, no hem, no interfacing
- **Shape**: no grain line (felt has no grain, so the pieces can be laid in any direction, though pressed felt may have a slightly stronger direction); SA 0 for a butted or lapped seam, 6 mm for an overlapped seam, 1 cm for a plain seam pressed open (typical; ST section 1.1 lapped seam). Edges are cut and left raw. Shaping by steam allows hats and bags to be moulded. Corners need rounded inside corners. Source: unverified.
- **Drafting**: SA 0 to 6 mm; omit hem; omit notches that cut deep (use small snips); keep curves gentle.
- **Sewing order**: overlap or butt the edges, topstitch or zigzag; hand blanket stitch for decorative edges.
- **Finishes and notions**: needle 80/12 to 90/14, polyester thread, stitch 3 mm, steam shaping; felt does not fray, so no edge finish.
- **Fabrics**: 100 percent wool felt (1 to 3 mm for crafts) is firmer than acrylic felt; wool felt shrinks and shapes when wet; boiled wool is a knitted wool fulled (felted) after knitting, it is not felt.
- **Fit faults**: seam stretches in curves -> stabilise; felt pills -> use wool.
- **Variants**: #leather-lambskin-cowhide (the same lapped-seam logic).
- **CLO3D**: no grain fabric with high stiffness and low stretch; seams lapped or butted.
- **Confidence**: trade practice; allowances typical.

## Fibre properties {#fibre-properties}

- **Aliases**: fibre choice, fibre behaviour; fr: propriétés des fibres, fibres textiles
- **Family**: materials
- **Recognize**: fibre content label (cotton, linen, wool, silk, viscose, polyester, nylon, elastane); burn test or feel when unlabelled
- **Pieces**: not applicable; the fibre sets ease, drape, SA finish and pressing
- **Shape**: how each fibre acts on the pattern (qualitative, trade practice). **Cotton**: absorbent, stable, wrinkles, shrinks in the first wash. **Linen**: stiff, strongest of the natural cellulose fibres, wrinkles badly, frays, shrinks (#linen-sewing). **Wool**: elastic, resilient, absorbs moisture, takes steam and shaping, felts with heat and agitation (#wool-suiting-sewing, #shrink-stretch-iron). **Silk**: strong, fine, lustrous, slips, marks with water (#silk-satin-sewing). **Viscose/rayon**: soft drape, weak when wet, shrinks and ravels. **Acetate**: drape, heat-sensitive. **Polyester**: strong, wrinkle-resistant, heat-settable, low shrinkage, melts under a hot iron. **Nylon**: very strong, abrasion-resistant, melts, used for linings, shells, hosiery. **Elastane (spandex, Lycra)**: stretch several times its length, degraded by heat and chlorine, always blended (#power-mesh-lycra-sewing). **Lyocell and modal**: smooth drape, strong. Source: unverified (general textile knowledge; no pattern value is derived from this entry).
- **Drafting**: ease and drape follow the fabric, not the fibre alone; test the weave and weight. No numbers here.
- **Sewing order**: not applicable.
- **Finishes and notions**: iron temperature by fibre (ST section 7: silk, viscose, acetate 110 to 130 C; polyester, nylon 110 to 150 C; wool 148 to 160 C; cotton 150 to 200 C; linen 200 C).
- **Fabrics**: blends take the temperature of the most sensitive fibre; check the label first.
- **Fit faults**: garment shrinks -> pre-treat (#shrinkage-pretreatment); shine after pressing -> wool and acetate need a cloth.
- **Variants**: #weaves-plain-twill-satin-jacquard, #knit-structures.
- **CLO3D**: fibre enters only through the physical properties (weight, bending stiffness, stretch, thickness).
- **Confidence**: from memory (standard textile references); temperatures from ST.

## Weaves: plain, twill, satin and jacquard {#weaves-plain-twill-satin-jacquard}

- **Aliases**: plain weave, tabby, twill, sateen, satin weave, jacquard, brocade, damask; fr: toile, sergé, satin, jacquard
- **Family**: materials
- **Recognize**: plain weave (1 over, 1 under; same on both sides: poplin, lawn, chiffon, organza, canvas); twill (diagonal rib; denim, gabardine, drill, tweed, herringbone); satin (long floats on the face, smooth and lustrous, with a duller back; sateen is weft-faced cotton); jacquard (figured weave made on a jacquard loom: brocade, damask, matelasse, with large patterns)
- **Pieces**: not applicable
- **Shape**: the weave decides how the pattern is laid. Plain weave: balanced, firm, frays easily, little bias stretch; twill: drapes better than plain, diagonal line has a direction (cut all pieces in the same twill direction, see #denim-sewing), frays less; satin: floats snag and shift, seams slip (wider SA 1.5 cm, #silk-satin-sewing); jacquard: motif directions and repeat distances must be matched at seams and set in the layout (more fabric: repeat length per piece), often one-way. Source: unverified (general textile knowledge).
- **Drafting**: for patterned weaves add the repeat to the layout and mark the matching points (notches at the same place on the motif).
- **Sewing order**: not applicable.
- **Finishes and notions**: loosely woven fabrics need underlining or wider seam finishes (#underlining); twills take flat-felled seams.
- **Fabrics**: examples in the Recognize field.
- **Fit faults**: fabric twists after washing -> off-grain cutting; motifs do not match -> cut single layer and match.
- **Variants**: #grain-and-bias, #nap-and-one-way-layout.
- **CLO3D**: weave appears as a texture map and as the warp/weft stiffness and stretch values; twill direction is a texture rotation.
- **Confidence**: from memory (standard textile references).

## Knit structures: weft and warp knits {#knit-structures}

- **Aliases**: weft knit, warp knit, single jersey, rib, interlock, purl, tricot, raschel, wale, course; fr: tricot à mailles jetées, maille cueillie, chaîne
- **Family**: materials
- **Recognize**: weft knits (jersey, rib, interlock, purl, sweater knits, fleece) are made from one yarn looping across the width; they ladder or run, curl at the edge (single jersey) and stretch more across than along. Warp knits (tricot, raschel, power mesh, locknit lingerie fabrics) are made from many yarns running along the length; they do not ladder, do not curl as much, and are more stable in length
- **Pieces**: not applicable
- **Shape**: wales run lengthwise (columns of loops), courses run crosswise (rows). Cut pieces with the wale along the garment length (the grain line is the wale) so the greater stretch goes round the body. Single jersey has a right and a wrong side and curls; rib is symmetrical and very stretchy crosswise (80 to 150 percent as a band, KL section 1); interlock is two interlocked ribs, smooth on both sides, stable and non-curling. Source: unverified (general textile knowledge); stretch classes from KL.
- **Drafting**: put the grain line along the wale; measure the stretch in both directions (#stretch-measurement).
- **Sewing order**: not applicable.
- **Finishes and notions**: needles and stitches per #stretch-knits-sewing.
- **Fabrics**: examples above.
- **Fit faults**: garment twists -> cut off the wale; garment grows in wear -> poor recovery, stabilise.
- **Variants**: #stretch-knits-sewing, #power-mesh-lycra-sewing.
- **CLO3D**: set stretch weft and warp separately; knit fabrics need a finer mesh.
- **Confidence**: from memory; stretch classes verified in KL.

## Grain and bias {#grain-and-bias}

- **Aliases**: straight grain, cross grain, true bias, grain line, selvedge; fr: droit-fil, travers, biais, lisière
- **Family**: materials
- **Recognize**: in a woven, warp yarns run along the selvedge (lengthwise grain), weft yarns across (crosswise grain), and the bias is the diagonal at 45 degrees. The grain line arrow on a pattern piece is parallel to the lengthwise grain
- **Pieces**: not applicable
- **Shape**: lengthwise grain is stable, strong, with almost no stretch; crosswise grain has slightly more give; bias has the most stretch, drapes and clings, frays less. A garment hangs correctly only when the grain line stays vertical (CF and CB on the straight grain, a hem on a bias skirt needs a 24 hour hang, ST section 12). Bias strips for binding: width finished plus allowances (ST section 1.1: 2.5 to 3 cm for a 6 mm Hong Kong binding). A bias seam stretches while sewn: stabilise it with stay tape or organza (#silk-organza). Source: unverified (trade practice).
- **Drafting**: draw the grain line on every piece; for bias cutting rotate the piece 45 degrees and mark the line; make the pattern with the CF on the bias carefully because the seam stretches.
- **Sewing order**: stay-stitch bias edges; hang bias garments before hemming.
- **Finishes and notions**: stay tape, silk organza stay.
- **Fabrics**: all wovens; knits have wales and courses instead (#knit-structures).
- **Fit faults**: garment twists or hangs crooked -> off-grain cutting; hem dips on bias -> hang and re-mark.
- **Variants**: #nap-and-one-way-layout.
- **CLO3D**: grain direction is set with `set_grain_direction`; bias pieces use a rotated grain.
- **Confidence**: trade practice.

## Nap and one-way layout {#nap-and-one-way-layout}

- **Aliases**: with nap, one-way layout, directional fabric, pile direction, directional print; fr: sens du poil, sens unique, sens du tissu
- **Family**: materials
- **Recognize**: fabrics whose face looks different from the two ends: velvet, corduroy, velveteen, fleece, brushed flannel, faux fur, suede, twill with a strong diagonal, satin and shot silk, directional prints (floral with a top and bottom), stripes and plaids with an uneven repeat
- **Pieces**: not applicable
- **Shape**: all pattern pieces are cut with the same direction (nap arrow points the same way on every piece). In a layout this means the pieces cannot be placed head to toe, so the fabric consumption rises (typical estimate 10 to 15 percent more than a with-or-without layout; verify per marker). Nap up (pile brushed upward) shows a deeper colour, nap down a lighter colour with more sheen (#velvet-pile). Plaids need the repeat matched at CF, side seams and across sleeves: cut single layer; add the repeat length per large piece. Source: unverified (trade practice; fabric increase is an estimate).
- **Drafting**: add a nap arrow to every piece; note on the pattern "with nap".
- **Sewing order**: not applicable.
- **Finishes and notions**: note on the cutting layout.
- **Fabrics**: examples above.
- **Fit faults**: shade difference between pieces -> pieces cut in different directions.
- **Variants**: #grain-and-bias, #fur-faux-fur-sewing.
- **CLO3D**: set the grain/pile direction on each piece; a one-way texture must be rotated with each piece.
- **Confidence**: trade practice.

## Measuring stretch {#stretch-measurement}

- **Aliases**: stretch test, stretch percentage, recovery test; fr: mesure de l'élasticité, test d'étirement
- **Family**: materials
- **Recognize**: needed for every knit and stretch woven before drafting negative ease
- **Pieces**: not applicable
- **Shape**: the stretch percentage is the single number that sets the ease and the band ratios. Test (KL section 1.1): mark 10 cm on a straight swatch at least 20 cm wide, stretch to the comfortable maximum (the point at which the knit opens or resists hard), read L1, stretch % = (L1 - L0) / L0 x 100; release for a minute: recovery should return to within 2 percent of L0, and more than 5 percent growth means poor recovery. Repeat across the width and along the length. Classes (KL section 1.2): stable 18 to 25 percent; moderate 26 to 50; high 51 to 75; super 76 to 100; rib as a band 80 to 150. The garment uses only a fraction of the stretch at rest: 0.1 to 0.25 for body-skimming, 0.25 to 0.5 for close fit and swimwear, never above 0.5 (KL section 1.3). Source: drawn from the KL sheet, which cites Chalk & Notch, Siemacht and ASG.
- **Drafting**: pattern reduction = u x stretch percent, where u is the fraction used; measure on the washed fabric.
- **Sewing order**: not applicable.
- **Finishes and notions**: stretch-compatible needle and stitch (#stretch-knits-sewing).
- **Fabrics**: woven fabrics with elastane (stretch denim 1 to 3 percent elastane, stretch cotton) follow the same test.
- **Fit faults**: garment loose after wear -> poor recovery; garment tight -> stretch measured on the unwashed fabric.
- **Variants**: #knit-structures, #power-mesh-lycra-sewing.
- **CLO3D**: enter the measured stretch in the fabric properties (weft, warp); see `clo3d.md`.
- **Confidence**: verified in KL (sources cited there); u rule as given there.

## Shrinkage and pre-treatment {#shrinkage-pretreatment}

- **Aliases**: pre-washing, pre-shrinking, decatising, sponging, relaxing; fr: pré-lavage, décatissage, rétractation
- **Family**: materials
- **Recognize**: needed before cutting any fabric that will be washed, or any wool that will be steamed
- **Pieces**: not applicable
- **Shape**: shrinkage changes the finished dimensions, so either the fabric is shrunk before cutting (preferred), or the pattern is enlarged. Test: cut a 20 x 20 cm swatch, treat it as the garment will be treated, measure again, shrinkage % = (before - after) / before x 100, in both directions. Typical orders of magnitude (from memory, varies strongly by fabric and finish): cotton and linen a few percent in the first wash (linen up to 10 percent in loose weaves, see #linen-sewing), cotton jersey 5 to 10 percent mostly in length, raw denim 3 to 8 percent, wool a small amount in a steam press (decatise before cutting), synthetics very little. Source: unverified (general practice).
- **Drafting**: if the fabric is not pre-shrunk, add the measured percentage to length and width; the safer choice is to pre-treat.
- **Sewing order**: pre-treat the fabric, the interfacing (steam woven fusibles and test fuse) and the lining before cutting.
- **Finishes and notions**: wool: steam from the wrong side with a cloth or have it decatised by a cleaner; silk: test a swatch for dry-cleaning only; washable fabrics: wash and dry as the garment will be treated.
- **Fabrics**: fabrics labelled "pre-shrunk" or "sanforized" still shrink 1 to 2 percent (from memory).
- **Fit faults**: garment shrinks after washing -> pre-treat; lining shrinks differently from shell -> pre-treat both.
- **Variants**: #fibre-properties.
- **CLO3D**: shrinkage values per fabric in the fabric properties (`set_shrinkage`) are applied to the pattern pieces.
- **Confidence**: test method trade practice; numbers from memory.

## Fusible woven interfacing {#interfacing-fusible-woven}

- **Aliases**: fusible woven interfacing, woven fusible, cotton fusible; fr: thermocollant tissé, entoilage thermocollant tissé
- **Family**: interfacings
- **Recognize**: a woven cotton or polyester fabric with a dotted adhesive on one face; stable in both directions, follows the grain; light to heavy weights
- **Pieces**: collar, collar stand, cuffs, waistband strips, front bands, plackets, facings (cut from the pattern, minus 3 mm at the SA edges; ST section 4.6)
- **Shape**: cut from the same pattern as the piece, with the same grain direction, with 3 mm removed at the cut edge and at the fold so it does not enter the seam; strip interfacing at zips, hems, buttonholes. Source: unverified (rules from ST section 4.6).
- **Drafting**: trace the piece; reduce by 3 mm at the SA edges; mark the grain.
- **Sewing order**: fuse before the piece is joined: 130 to 150 C, 10 to 15 seconds, steam, no sliding, cool before moving (ST sections 4.6 and 9).
- **Finishes and notions**: press cloth; test on a scrap of the shell for strike-through and shrinkage.
- **Fabrics**: stable cotton and linen; do not fuse to loose weaves or heavy knits; shell fabric textures change after fusing.
- **Fit faults**: bubbles -> too hot or too short a press; stiff collar -> lighter weight.
- **Variants**: #interfacing-fusible-knit-nonwoven, #interfacing-weft-insertion, #interfacing-canvas-horsehair.
- **CLO3D**: add a second layer or increase the stiffness of the piece.
- **Confidence**: trade practice (ST section 4.6).

## Fusible knit (tricot) and non-woven interfacing {#interfacing-fusible-knit-nonwoven}

- **Aliases**: tricot interfacing, knit fusible, nonwoven fusible, stretch interfacing; fr: thermocollant maille, thermocollant non-tissé
- **Family**: interfacings
- **Recognize**: knit (tricot) interfacing is a warp-knit with a stretch across and stable in length; non-woven is a web of fibres with no grain; both fuse with a dotted adhesive
- **Pieces**: as for woven fusibles: collars, facings, hems, pocket openings, stretch areas
- **Shape**: knit fusible: lengthwise (the stable direction) along the grain line of the piece, its stretch lets the shell give; non-woven: no grain so can be cut in any direction (saves material), cheaper, stiffer in one pass, may bubble after washing; used for bags, stable areas, craft. Cut as in #interfacing-fusible-woven. Source: unverified (trade practice).
- **Drafting**: pattern minus 3 mm at the edges; use the knit's stable direction along the grain.
- **Sewing order**: fuse as for woven fusible, at lower temperature for knits.
- **Finishes and notions**: stretch interfacing for jersey necklines (a strip of fusible tape keeps shape without losing give).
- **Fabrics**: knits, stretch wovens, light silks (knit fusible), crafts and bags (non-woven).
- **Fit faults**: shell goes stiff -> choose a lighter knit; interfacing bubbles after washing -> low quality, pre-shrink.
- **Variants**: #interfacing-weft-insertion.
- **CLO3D**: not modelled.
- **Confidence**: trade practice.

## Weft-insertion interfacing {#interfacing-weft-insertion}

- **Aliases**: weft-inserted fusible, shirt interfacing, wefted knit interfacing; fr: thermocollant trame, entoilage trame insérée
- **Family**: interfacings
- **Recognize**: a light tricot-like knit base with weft yarns inserted across; stable across the width, slight give along the length; very soft hand
- **Pieces**: shirt collars and cuffs, blouse fronts, silky fabrics, light wool
- **Shape**: cut with the inserted yarns across (perpendicular to the grain line of the piece) or along the shell grain as the maker states; marked on the interfacing roll (check the supplier's note). Pattern edges as #interfacing-fusible-woven. Source: unverified.
- **Drafting**: pattern minus 3 mm; check the grain.
- **Sewing order**: fuse as for woven fusibles.
- **Finishes and notions**: used instead of woven fusibles when softness and stability are both needed.
- **Fabrics**: silk, viscose, fine cotton, wool crepe.
- **Fit faults**: collar tip curls -> wrong direction or hot press.
- **Variants**: #interfacing-fusible-woven.
- **CLO3D**: not modelled.
- **Confidence**: from memory.

## Hair canvas, horsehair and organza (sew-in interfacings) {#interfacing-canvas-horsehair}

- **Aliases**: tailor's canvas, French canvas, hymo, horsehair canvas, hair canvas, silk organza interfacing, muslin; fr: toile tailleur, crin, toile de crin, organza
- **Family**: interfacings
- **Recognize**: sew-in interfacings for tailoring and couture; hair canvas has a cotton warp and a weft of wool and goat hair, soft and springy; horsehair canvas has horsehair in the weft, stiff and used for shoulders, chest pieces, hems; silk organza is a fine, crisp, transparent plain-weave interfacing for blouses, collars and bodices
- **Pieces**: front canvas, chest piece, collar canvas (#canvas-fronts, #under-collar-shaping), hem strip (#hem-couture)
- **Shape**: canvas is cut with the straight grain along the front edge, the bias cut for chest pieces and collar canvas; hem strips are cut on the bias 4 to 6 cm wide (ST section 4.6). Weights: use a hair canvas of medium weight for jackets, a lighter weight for light wool, horsehair for shaped structures. Source: unverified (trade practice; details in the tailoring sheets).
- **Drafting**: trace the net lines for the canvas; omit the SA at the front edge; mark the roll line.
- **Sewing order**: baste and pad stitch (#pad-stitching), catch-stitch the edges (#catch-stitch).
- **Finishes and notions**: linen tape, bias strip for hems, silk thread.
- **Fabrics**: wool, tweed; silk organza for silks and fine cottons.
- **Fit faults**: lapel is limp -> canvas too light; shell cockles -> canvas shrinks, pre-shrink it.
- **Variants**: #interfacing-fusible-woven, #silk-organza.
- **CLO3D**: not modelled.
- **Confidence**: trade practice.

## Linings {#linings}

- **Aliases**: jacket lining, coat lining, skirt lining, bemberg, cupro, acetate, polyester taffeta, viscose, silk habotai; fr: doublure, doublure de veste
- **Family**: materials
- **Recognize**: smooth fabric inside a garment, finished to the facings; in jackets the back lining has a centre-back pleat (a fold of ease) and the hem hangs free with a jump hem
- **Pieces**: lining front (2), back (2 or 1 on a fold with pleat), sleeve lining (2), pocket bags (4), facing (shell)
- **Shape**: per ST section 7: lining is cut from the garment pattern; the front facing width is removed from the front edge; a CB pleat of 1.5 to 2 cm (fold) gives ease; add 1 cm at the shoulder and in the sleeve length; the hem is 1.5 to 2.5 cm shorter than the garment (jump hem fold); a smaller sleeve cap ease than the shell. A bagged lining leaves a 20 cm opening in the sleeve lining. Skirt lining is 3 to 4 cm shorter, with darts or smaller. Source: ST section 7; no drawn outline examined.
- **Drafting**: trace the garment pattern pieces at the net line; subtract the facing width at CF; add the CB pleat; add the ease at the shoulder and sleeve length; shorten at the hem; add 1.5 cm SA.
- **Sewing order**: lining is sewn to the facing, the sleeves attached with ease, the hem hand-felled.
- **Finishes and notions**: cupro (Bemberg) is breathable and anti-static; acetate is cheap; polyester taffeta is durable but not breathable; silk habotai is luxury; stretch lining for fitted jackets.
- **Fabrics**: lightweight and smooth; match care with the shell.
- **Fit faults**: lining pulls the hem up -> hem too short or too tight; lining drags in the sleeve -> not enough ease.
- **Variants**: #underlining, interlining (ST section 7).
- **CLO3D**: add a second layer pattern or a lining pattern sewn to the facing; set it as a thin, slippery fabric.
- **Confidence**: trade practice (ST section 7).

## Zips (types and sizes) {#zips-notions}

- **Aliases**: zippers, coil zip, metal zip, plastic moulded zip, invisible zip, separating zip, two-way zip; fr: fermetures à glissière, fermeture éclair
- **Family**: notions
- **Recognize**: coil (nylon or polyester coil, flexible, light; the usual dress zip); metal (strong, heavy, jeans and jackets); moulded plastic (Vislon; chunky teeth, sportswear); invisible (coil hidden behind the tape, pulls at the top); separating (open end, jackets); two-way (double sliders); size is the closed-teeth width in mm (3 for light, 5 for general use, 8 to 10 for heavy and outerwear; YKK convention, from memory)
- **Pieces**: zip tape, facing or placket strip at the zip seam
- **Shape**: install rules in ST section 5.1: opening length = zip length minus 1 cm (stops 1 to 1.5 cm under the waist seam); SA 1.5 to 2 cm; separating zips have the facing turned over the tape. Zip lengths by use: skirt 18 to 22 cm, trousers fly 11 to 18 cm, neckline 18 to 25 cm, sleeve 8 to 10 cm (ST). Source: ST section 5.1.
- **Drafting**: see ST section 5.1 for allowances; mark the zip stop with a notch.
- **Sewing order**: see ST section 5.1 and #hand-picked-zip.
- **Finishes and notions**: zipper foot, invisible zip foot (#presser-feet), hook and eye above the zip.
- **Fabrics**: invisible zips need a soft fabric; metal zips on heavy fabrics.
- **Fit faults**: zip waves -> tape stretched on installation; zip does not close -> too tight at hips.
- **Variants**: #hand-picked-zip.
- **CLO3D**: zipper trim in the GUI; `set_zipper_style` sets the style; placement by hand.
- **Confidence**: trade practice and ST; size convention from memory.

## Buttons, snaps and hooks {#buttons-snaps-hooks}

- **Aliases**: buttons, shank buttons, sew-through buttons, snap fasteners, press studs, hook and eye, trouser hook and bar; fr: boutons, pressions, agrafes, crochets
- **Family**: notions
- **Recognize**: sew-through buttons (2 or 4 holes) lie flat; shank buttons (loop on the back) stand off thick fabric; covered buttons; toggles; sew-on snaps (hidden), press studs (visible dome, tool applied), hooks and eyes, hooks and bars for waistbands
- **Pieces**: button strip or placket with interfacing; buttonholes; button stand
- **Shape**: buttonhole length = button diameter + thickness (ST section 5.2); overlap for snaps and hooks 1.5 to 2.5 cm; waistband extension for a trouser hook and bar 3 to 3.5 cm; snaps spaced 5 to 8 cm; sizes: snap 0 to 3 (about 8 to 12 mm) for light fabrics, press studs 15 to 18 mm for heavy (ST section 5.3). Typical button diameters: shirts 10 to 12 mm, jackets and coats 20 to 34 mm (from memory). Source: ST sections 5.2 and 5.3.
- **Drafting**: place buttons on the pattern with an extension equal to the overlap; the buttonhole sits perpendicular to the edge (horizontal) at the centre front.
- **Sewing order**: interface the area, stitch buttonholes, sew buttons with a thread shank on thick fabric.
- **Finishes and notions**: button thread or doubled polyester; a reinforcing button inside on thin fabrics.
- **Fabrics**: heavy fabrics need a shank; leather uses press studs or bound buttonholes.
- **Fit faults**: gaping between buttons -> add buttons or reduce spacing (ST section 12 notes ASG on gaping).
- **Variants**: #machine-buttonhole.
- **CLO3D**: buttons and buttonholes through the pattern JSON (see `clo3d.md`); `set_button_colors` for colour.
- **Confidence**: ST; button diameters from memory.

## Elastics {#elastics-notions}

- **Aliases**: braided elastic, woven elastic, knit elastic, clear elastic, fold-over elastic (FOE), plush-back elastic, swim elastic, shirring elastic; fr: élastiques, élastique tressé, élastique tricoté, élastique transparent
- **Family**: notions
- **Recognize**: braided elastic narrows when stretched and is for casings; woven elastic keeps its width and does not roll, used in waistbands; knit elastic is soft and used for lingerie and active; clear polyurethane elastic stabilises seams; fold-over elastic finishes edges; shirring elastic thread gathers fabric
- **Pieces**: casing or direct application
- **Shape**: lengths per ST section 4.4: waistband elastic 0.8 to 0.9 of the waist measure (plus 2.5 cm overlap); swim leg 0.8 to 0.9 of the opening; lingerie 0.8 to 0.9; clear elastic 0.75 to 0.9 of the seam; casing width = elastic width + 3 to 6 mm; for swimwear edge elastic 85 to 95 percent (KL section 6.1). Source: ST section 4.4 and KL.
- **Drafting**: see ST section 4.4.
- **Sewing order**: see ST section 4.4.
- **Finishes and notions**: 3-step zigzag or coverstitch (#stitch-602); chlorine-resistant swim elastic.
- **Fabrics**: braided elastic loses power when sewn through; use woven elastic for sewn-through.
- **Fit faults**: waistband loose -> elastic too long or poor recovery; casing twists -> flatten or use woven elastic.
- **Variants**: #power-mesh-lycra-sewing.
- **CLO3D**: `set_elastic` for an edge elastic ratio (0.75 to 0.9) on outline lines.
- **Confidence**: ST and KL.

## Boning {#boning-notions}

- **Aliases**: corset boning, spiral steel, flat steel, plastic boning, Rigilene, polyester boning, bone casing; fr: baleines, baleine acier spiralée, baleine plate
- **Family**: notions
- **Recognize**: rigid strips in channels at princess seams, side seams, CF, CB; flat steel is rigid vertically; spiral steel bends in both directions along curves; plastic boning is light and used on collars and bustiers
- **Pieces**: casings (channel tape) or channels formed by the seam allowances; ends capped
- **Shape**: channel width = bone width + 3 mm; bone length = seam length minus 1.5 cm at top and bottom (minus 2 cm at a waist seam); SA 1.5 to 2 cm on the seam with the bone; never at the neckline edge (leave 1 to 1.5 cm); plastic cut 1 cm shorter than the channel with rounded ends (ST section 4.5). Source: ST section 4.5.
- **Drafting**: mark bone positions at the seams on the pattern, with bone length and a note on the channel width.
- **Sewing order**: stitch channel, insert, close ends.
- **Finishes and notions**: bone tips or caps, waist tape, busk or CF bones (ST section 4.5).
- **Fabrics**: strong, stable fabrics; add a strength layer (coutil, canvas, duck).
- **Fit faults**: bone pokes through -> rounded ends and caps; bone twists -> too narrow channel.
- **Variants**: corset in `../techniques/` (lingerie); #underlining.
- **CLO3D**: not modelled; use a stiffer strip (narrow pattern with high bending stiffness) at the channel.
- **Confidence**: ST section 4.5.

## Tapes and hook-and-loop {#tapes-velcro}

- **Aliases**: stay tape, twill tape, seam binding, bias tape, grosgrain, hook-and-eye tape, Velcro (hook and loop), webbing; fr: rubans, ganse, ruban de biais, bande auto-agrippante
- **Family**: notions
- **Recognize**: stay tape (woven or fusible, 6 to 12 mm) fixes stretching edges; twill tape stabilises seams and roll lines; seam binding and bias tape finish edges; hook-and-loop strips fasten overlapping parts; hook-and-eye tape is used in bras and corsets
- **Pieces**: tape strips, hook-and-loop strips (hook sewn to the outer layer facing the loop)
- **Shape**: stay tape length = the net seam line length (#silk-organza, ST section 7); roll line tape 0.6 to 1 cm shorter than the line (#roll-line-taping); hook-and-loop overlap 2.5 to 4 cm (ST section 5.3); hook-and-eye tape width 2 to 3 cm with spacing 1.5 to 2.5 cm; bias tape strip width as ST section 1.1 for the binding. Source: ST sections 1.1, 5.3, 7.
- **Drafting**: notch the tape ends; add overlap at closures.
- **Sewing order**: tape is stitched into the seam or on the seam allowance.
- **Finishes and notions**: sew hook-and-loop with a sharp needle and a long stitch; the hook side snags knits, keep it inside the overlap.
- **Fabrics**: avoid hook and loop on fine knits and lace.
- **Fit faults**: edge stretches -> tape too long; fastening opens -> overlap too small.
- **Variants**: #roll-line-taping, #hem-couture (hem tape).
- **CLO3D**: tapes via `set_seam_taping` where available; hook-and-loop not modelled.
- **Confidence**: ST.

## Pressing and finishing {#pressing-finishing}

- **Aliases**: pressing, ironing, steaming, clapper, ham, sleeve board, pressing cloth, tailor's board; fr: repassage, pressage, finition, carton, jeannette
- **Family**: techniques
- **Recognize**: press each seam as it is sewn; a pressed seam is flat and the shell has no shine or imprint
- **Pieces**: not applicable
- **Shape**: no effect on the pattern itself, except through shaping by heat (#shrink-stretch-iron). Temperature table in ST section 7: silk, viscose, acetate 110 to 130 C; polyester, nylon 110 to 150 C; wool 148 to 160 C; cotton 150 to 200 C; linen 200 C; velvet on a needleboard; knits 110 to 150 C. Rules: press flat first, then open or to one side, before crossing with another seam; use a ham for curves and a sleeve board for narrow tubes; press from the wrong side with a cloth; lift the iron on the bias; let the piece cool before moving it. Source: ST section 7.
- **Drafting**: not applicable.
- **Sewing order**: press after each seam; final press after the hem.
- **Finishes and notions**: clapper to flatten wool seams, tailor's ham, seam roll, point presser, wool pad for velvet, press cloth (silk organza is the couture press cloth: transparent, heat-resistant), tailor's board.
- **Fabrics**: leather, faux leather, sequins and coated fabrics: no steam or low heat (#leather-lambskin-cowhide, #waterproof-technical-fabrics).
- **Fit faults**: shine -> cloth and less heat; seam imprint (ridge) shows -> press over a seam roll or place paper under the allowance.
- **Variants**: #shrink-stretch-iron.
- **CLO3D**: not applicable.
- **Confidence**: ST section 7 (iron temperatures: check the fibre label first).
