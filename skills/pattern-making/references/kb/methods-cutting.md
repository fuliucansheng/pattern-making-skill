# Methods, drafting systems, fitting, grading and cutting

Reading notes for this file:

- "typical" marks a trade-practice value that varies by system, fabric or factory. It is never a
  guarantee. "not known" means no verified source was found.
- Drafting systems differ most in three places: how ease is built in, how the armhole and sleeve cap are
  proportioned, and whether lengths come from measurements or from fractions of the bust. When two systems
  are mixed (a bodice from one, a sleeve from another), always re-measure armhole against cap.
- A comparison study of Bunka, ESMOD, Armstrong and Aldrich fitted bodices (see Sources) found different
  silhouettes from the same body and attributed the differences to ease and dart allocation, not to error.

## Aldrich (Metric Pattern Cutting) {#aldrich-system}

- **Aliases**: Winifred Aldrich, Metric Pattern Cutting, British metric block system; fr: methode Aldrich
- **Family**: drafting system (measurement based, British, metric)
- **Recognize**: books "Metric Pattern Cutting for Women's Wear / Menswear / Children's Wear / Lingerie /
  Jersey Fashion". Blocks are drawn on a rectangle built from bust width and a set of vertical lines
  (waist, bust, armhole) and the bodice back and front are drawn side by side. Close-fitting, straight
  and easy-fitting variants are separate blocks.
- **Pieces**: close-fitting bodice (front 1, back 1), straight skirt (front 1, back 1), close-fitting
  sleeve (1, one-piece), trouser block, plus dress, jacket, coat blocks built from the same measurements
- **Shape**: bodice rectangle width = half bust + 5 cm (so full ease on the round is about 10 cm for the
  close-fitting bodice block when read as "width of back plus front"); armhole depth line is taken from
  the armhole depth measurement plus a small constant; back neck width and depth are fractions of the neck
  size; shoulder points from fixed constants plus shoulder length measurement. Front and back are shifted
  at the side seam using chest and back width. Source: drawn pattern: not seen; secondary instructions
  only, see Sources. Exact constants unverified.
- **Drafting**: 1) Take the measurements of the book's size chart (bust, waist, hip, nape to waist, armhole
  depth, neck size, shoulder, back width, chest, dart). 2) Draw the block rectangle with width = half bust
  + 5 cm (secondary sources) and lengths from nape to waist. 3) Add armhole, shoulder and neck from the
  book's constants. 4) Position darts by the book's dart table. 5) Check seams: armhole and cap lengths
  are tabulated, the sleeve is drafted against the measured armhole.
- **Sewing order**: not applicable (system, not a garment)
- **Finishes and notions**: block carries no seam allowance; the book adds them in the finishing chapters
- **Fabrics**: wovens of light and medium weight for the close-fitting block; separate ease charts for
  stretch (jersey volume)
- **Fit faults**: one reported case of too much underarm ease arose when the armhole depth measurement
  did not match what the book expects. Fix by re-measuring on the book's definition. Other faults: see
  fit-fault entries in this file.
- **Variants**: #armstrong-system (US, dart-led), #esmod-french-method, #bunka-system (lower armhole, more
  ease), #close-fitting-bodice-block
- **CLO3D**: draft in a CAD tool or import DXF; set seam allowance 0 for the block, add allowance after
  design changes; check armhole and cap lengths in the pattern measurements display.
- **Confidence**: system identity and the half-bust-plus-5 rule: trade practice, repeated in several
  secondary sources; all other constants: not verified, consult the book.

## Armstrong (Patternmaking for Fashion Design) {#armstrong-system}

- **Aliases**: Helen Joseph Armstrong, US flat pattern method, American dart-led sloper; fr: methode
  americaine Armstrong
- **Family**: drafting system (measurement and proportion mixed, US)
- **Recognize**: book "Patternmaking for Fashion Design". The bodice sloper has a bust dart plus a waist
  dart and a shoulder dart, with the dart pivoting at the bust point. Chapter order: slopers, dart
  manipulation, contouring, then design chapters.
- **Pieces**: bodice sloper (front 1, back 1), skirt sloper (front 1, back 1), sleeve sloper (1), pants
  sloper, plus extended slopers for dress, jacket, coat
- **Shape**: the bodice shows a stronger waist suppression than the Aldrich and ESMOD blocks in the
  comparison study (the study reports the most constricted silhouette and rounded hips for Armstrong).
  Anchor points are bust point, apex, armhole depth, shoulder tip. Ease is small; extra ease is added in
  each design chapter. Specific constants: not verified.
- **Drafting**: 1) Take measurements as the book defines (bust arc, waist arc, front and back lengths,
  shoulder, across chest, across back, apex position). 2) Draw front and back with the book's
  construction table. 3) Convert darts by pivot from the sloper. 4) Test on a toile.
- **Sewing order**: not applicable
- **Finishes and notions**: seam allowances are added at the end of each design, not on the sloper
- **Fabrics**: slopers are drafted for woven fabric; knit slopers are modified by a stretch percentage
- **Fit faults**: the book gives a fitting section after the instructions, including asymmetrical bodies
  (reported in a forum thread; verify in the book).
- **Variants**: #aldrich-system, #joseph-armstrong, #dart-pivot, #contouring-close-fit
- **CLO3D**: build the bodice with darts as sewn darts, or close darts and pivot in the 2D window; avatar
  fit check with tension map.
- **Confidence**: identity and silhouette difference: verified (comparison study abstract); constants: not
  known.

## Joseph-Armstrong {#joseph-armstrong}

- **Aliases**: Helen Joseph-Armstrong (same author as #armstrong-system), "Draping for Apparel Design",
  American draping and flat pattern teaching
- **Family**: drafting and draping system
- **Recognize**: the author's name is used with and without the hyphen. Her drafting book and her draping
  book use the same torso grid (CF, CB, bust line, waist line, hip line, balance lines), so a drape can
  be compared to a sloper directly.
- **Pieces**: dress form grid lines (CF, CB, side seam, bust, waist, hip), bodice and skirt drape blocks
- **Shape**: grid lines are horizontals and verticals marked with tape or pencil on muslin: lengthwise
  grain at CF and CB, crosswise grain at bust and hip. Source: unverified (from teaching practice).
- **Drafting**: 1) Mark grain lines on the muslin before draping. 2) Drape, mark and true. 3) Compare the
  drape to the flat sloper at the same grid lines. 4) Correct whichever one is wrong, not both.
- **Sewing order**: not applicable
- **Finishes and notions**: pins, tape, pencil, tracing wheel
- **Fabrics**: muslin of similar weight to the final fabric
- **Fit faults**: none specific
- **Variants**: #bodice-drape, #skirt-drape, #armstrong-system
- **CLO3D**: not applicable (physical drape); a 3D garment on an avatar can replace a drape for volume
  tests.
- **Confidence**: trade practice. Whether the hyphenated name denotes a later edition: not known.

## Muller & Sohn system {#mueller-sohn-system}

- **Aliases**: M. Muller & Sohn, Mueller und Sohn, Muller-Sohn Zuschneidesystem; fr: systeme Muller et
  Fils
- **Family**: drafting system (German, proportional, bust and measured lengths)
- **Recognize**: German books "Schnittkonstruktionen fuer Kleider und Blusen / Jacken und Maentel /
  Hosen / Herren" published by Rundschau-Verlag (the same publishing house as the Rundschau journals).
  Construction uses a fixed grid of vertical and horizontal lines labelled with letters, with sizes in
  German "Konfektionsgroessen".
- **Pieces**: Grundschnitt (basic block) for bodice, skirt, sleeve, trousers, coat; Modellschnitt (design
  patterns) derived from it
- **Shape**: the company's history page states the system originated in Munich and peaked in the 1920s
  with branches in several German cities, Vienna and Barcelona. Exact constants: not known; the book is
  required. Source: no drawn pattern seen.
- **Drafting**: 1) Choose German size, take the book's measurement table. 2) Build the Grundschnitt from
  the table (figures are tabulated per size in the book). 3) Develop Modellschnitt by the book's
  transformation chapters. 4) A published basic bodice in size 40 with specified wearing ease is sold
  digitally by the publisher.
- **Sewing order**: not applicable
- **Finishes and notions**: seam allowances added after design
- **Fabrics**: woven fashion fabrics, separate chapters for jackets and coats
- **Fit faults**: use the fit-fault entries; the book's corrections: not seen
- **Variants**: #rundschau-system, #dresdner-tailoring, #kunick-menswear, #aldrich-system
- **CLO3D**: import DXF from the publisher's digital patterns if purchased; otherwise rebuild by hand.
- **Confidence**: history and book list: verified (publisher and library listings); constants: not known.

## Bunka (Japanese bodice sloper) {#bunka-system}

- **Aliases**: Bunka Fashion College, Bunka fukuso gakuin method, Japanese sloper, "genkei" (Japanese
  for base pattern); fr: base japonaise Bunka
- **Family**: drafting system (Japanese, proportional to bust, derived from body-scan averages)
- **Recognize**: the base pattern of the "Pattern Magic" books by Tomoko Nakamichi. Armhole is lower, cap
  lower, armhole wider than most Western blocks; slight sway back and rounded shoulders built into the
  shape; one bust dart or waist darts with the same apex.
- **Pieces**: bodice (front 1, back 1), sleeve (1), skirt, trousers, each in Japanese sizes (S, M, L)
- **Shape**: Pattern Magic uses an adult women's size M: bust 83 cm, waist 64 cm, centre back length 38
  cm (reported on a blog about the sloper; verify in the books). Proportional formulas commonly cited for
  the Bunka bodice: armhole depth = bust / 12 + 13.7 cm; neck width = bust / 24 + 3.4 cm; ease about 12
  cm on the bust round and 6 cm on the waist. These are from memory and not verified on the page: check
  with a Bunka text. Source: unverified (from memory), the comparison study confirms lower armhole, wider
  armhole width and lower cap height than the Italian Secoli system, and a bodice that is slightly
  constricted at the waist.
- **Drafting**: 1) Measure bust, waist, hip, back length, shoulder. 2) Compute the bust-proportional
  lines. 3) Draw the bodice, shoulder and armhole. 4) Split the waist difference between darts. 5) Draft
  the sleeve against the measured armhole.
- **Sewing order**: not applicable
- **Finishes and notions**: seam allowance added after; some books give 1 cm (typical), others 1.5 cm
- **Fabrics**: woven; stretch variations are made by negative ease
- **Fit faults**: for non-Japanese figures, taller and rounder, the usual corrections are length of back,
  bust width and shoulder slope.
- **Variants**: #aldrich-system, #armstrong-system, #esmod-french-method, #close-fitting-bodice-block
- **CLO3D**: scale lengths for the avatar; Bunka sloper requires lower armhole in a 3D tight-fit test.
- **Confidence**: sources: comparison study and blog (verified); formulas: from memory.

## ESMOD / French method {#esmod-french-method}

- **Aliases**: methode ESMOD, methode francaise, patron de base; fr: "base corsage", "ligne de carrure",
  "ligne de poitrine"
- **Family**: drafting system (French, ease-led, taught at ESMOD schools)
- **Recognize**: construction starts with CB line, then horizontal lines (back length, armhole, bust,
  waist) and a "carrure" (across back) line; the front is drawn after the back; the dart is placed at
  the waist and the bust point; the bodice looks straighter than the Armstrong sloper.
- **Pieces**: base bodice (front, back), skirt, sleeve, trousers; derived design bases (casaque, robe)
- **Shape**: the comparison study reports ESMOD with a straight silhouette and less waist constriction.
  Typical drafting uses a quarter of bust plus ease per quarter. Constants: not verified.
- **Drafting**: 1) Take body measurements, bust, waist, hip, back length, front length, shoulder. 2) Draw
  CB and the horizontal levels. 3) Compute width from quarter bust plus ease. 4) Draw armhole through the
  across-back line. 5) Place darts and test.
- **Sewing order**: not applicable
- **Finishes and notions**: seam allowance 1 cm (typical) after the block
- **Fabrics**: wovens; the school also teaches knit adaptations
- **Fit faults**: see fit-fault entries
- **Variants**: #aldrich-system, #armstrong-system, #bunka-system
- **CLO3D**: as other blocks
- **Confidence**: identity: verified; constants: from memory, not stated.

## Dresdner / German tailoring {#dresdner-tailoring}

- **Aliases**: Dresdner Schnitt, German bespoke tailoring cut, Zuschneiden nach Mass; fr: coupe allemande
- **Family**: drafting tradition (menswear tailoring, German)
- **Recognize**: bespoke tailoring practice with chest-proportional drafts on paper with a tailor's square,
  shaped by hand and rework on a fitting. A specific Dresden school as a system: not found in sources.
- **Pieces**: coat/jacket (forepart, side body, back, undercollar, upper collar, sleeve upper and under
  sleeve), trousers; see tailoring-outerwear for the details already in the skill.
- **Shape**: not known as a named system
- **Drafting**: drafts are made from chest circumference fractions, with constants for armhole depth and
  neck; verified constants: not known. German publications by Rundschau-Verlag cover jackets and trousers.
- **Sewing order**: tailor's canvas, chest piece, padstitching, hand-finished lapels (trade practice)
- **Finishes and notions**: canvas, collar felt, shoulder pads, taped edges
- **Fabrics**: worsted wool suitings, flannel, tweed
- **Fit faults**: bespoke fitting corrections are made on the baste fitting
- **Variants**: #mueller-sohn-system, #rundschau-system, #jacket-block
- **CLO3D**: lapel roll lines must be set by hand; see jacket-block
- **Confidence**: tradition: trade practice; "Dresdner" as a distinct documented system: not known.

## Close-fitting bodice block {#close-fitting-bodice-block}

- **Aliases**: fitted bodice sloper, basic bodice block, waisted bodice; fr: corsage ajuste, base
  corsage
- **Family**: block (bodice)
- **Recognize**: back and front pieces with bust and waist darts; the shaped side seam; low ease; a
  front bust dart that points to the apex.
- **Pieces**: bodice front (cut 1 on fold, half), bodice back (1, half), sleeve (1) optional
- **Shape**: bust round plus 8 cm total ease is the skill's women default (see ease.md). The Aldrich close
  block uses half bust + 5 cm width, hence about 10 cm round. Waist ease 4 cm (skill default); shoulder
  slope 4 cm back, 5 cm front (blocks.md). Neck width bust/5 minus 0.2 back (blocks.md). Source: the
  numbers above come from the skill's blocks.md and from secondary Aldrich instructions.
- **Drafting**: 1) Draw back first with back length and armhole depth. 2) Draw front with the bust apex
  and bust dart. 3) Place waist darts. 4) Check shoulder seam length equal front and back, plus 0.5 to 1
  cm ease at the back. 5) Check armhole length. 6) Draw the sleeve.
- **Sewing order**: shoulder darts, bust dart, waist darts, shoulders, side seams, sleeves
- **Finishes and notions**: neckline facing or binding, zip or buttons in CB or CF
- **Fabrics**: wovens of light weight and low stretch; knit would take the knit block instead
- **Fit faults**: see fit-fault entries
- **Variants**: #easy-fitting-block, #shirt-block, #dress-block, #knit-block
- **CLO3D**: shape the darts as sewn darts or use an internal line; test on avatar with the correct size.
- **Confidence**: values from the skill's own references: trade practice.

## Easy-fitting block {#easy-fitting-block}

- **Aliases**: loose bodice block, semi-fitted block, "straight" block; fr: base ajustee ample
- **Family**: block (bodice, dress)
- **Recognize**: straighter side seams, fewer darts or no waist shaping, wider armhole.
- **Pieces**: front (1), back (1), sleeve (1)
- **Shape**: more ease than the close-fitting block. Skill guide: fitted blouse 8 to 10 cm, classic shirt 12
  to 16 cm, tailored jacket 10 to 14 cm bust ease on the full round (ease.md). The armhole is lowered and
  widened as ease increases.
- **Drafting**: 1) Start from the close-fitting block or draft with the larger ease. 2) Spread width at
  the side seam. 3) Lower the underarm line by about 1 to 2 cm per 4 cm extra bust ease (typical, not
  verified). 4) Re-draft the sleeve to the new armhole.
- **Sewing order**: as the base garment
- **Finishes and notions**: as base garment
- **Fabrics**: wovens, fine wools, viscose; if drapey, lower the ease
- **Fit faults**: if too wide at the shoulder, reduce shoulder width
- **Variants**: #close-fitting-bodice-block, #shirt-block, #jacket-block
- **CLO3D**: allow gravity; ease shows as drape.
- **Confidence**: ease values: skill references; armhole rule: typical, not verified.

## Kimono block {#kimono-block}

- **Aliases**: kimono sleeve block, cut-on sleeve, batwing block; fr: base kimono, manche kimono
- **Family**: block (bodice with sleeve cut in one)
- **Recognize**: body and sleeve in one piece, no armhole seam, shoulder line continues into the sleeve.
- **Pieces**: front (1), back (1), optional underarm gusset (2)
- **Shape**: shoulder line extended along a slope (angle chosen from design) to the sleeve length; the
  underarm curve begins about 2 to 4 cm below the armhole on a close version (typical). Without a gusset the
  arm lift is limited. Source: unverified (trade practice).
- **Drafting**: 1) Take the body block. 2) Extend the shoulder line by the sleeve length at chosen slope.
  3) Lower the underarm by the amount of arm lift needed. 4) Draw the sleeve hem. 5) Match front and back
  edges.
- **Sewing order**: shoulder, underarm and side seam in one pass, sleeve hem
- **Finishes and notions**: gusset (if any), sleeve hem
- **Fabrics**: fluid wovens, jersey; stiff fabrics restrict arm movement
- **Fit faults**: drag lines at underarm: add gusset or lower the underarm
- **Variants**: #kimono-from-block-gusset, #dropped-shoulder
- **CLO3D**: pieces may need a bend line for arm; place on avatar with arms in A pose.
- **Confidence**: trade practice.

## Shirt block {#shirt-block}

- **Aliases**: dress shirt block, blouse block; fr: base chemise
- **Family**: block (upper-body, woven)
- **Recognize**: yoke, front with button stand, low cap sleeve with placket and cuff.
- **Pieces**: front (2), back (1 on fold), yoke (2), sleeve (2), cuff (4 incl. interfacing), collar stand
  (2), collar (2)
- **Shape**: skill guide: 12 to 16 cm of bust ease, low sleeve cap 10 to 13 cm, yoke cut from a horizontal
  line (blocks.md). Center front extension equals the button diameter. Source: skill references.
- **Drafting**: 1) Men's or women's block with shirt ease. 2) Yoke line. 3) Shoulder dart into yoke. 4)
  Back pleat or gathers at yoke. 5) Cuff and collar.
- **Sewing order**: yoke, fronts, shoulders, collar, sleeves, side seams, cuffs, hem
- **Finishes and notions**: flat-felled or topstitched seams, interfacing in collar, stand and cuffs,
  buttons
- **Fabrics**: poplin, oxford, twill
- **Fit faults**: collar gape, cuff tightness; fix by neck size and cuff measure
- **Variants**: #easy-fitting-block, #yoke-creation
- **CLO3D**: sewing a collar stand needs ordered seams
- **Confidence**: skill references.

## Dress block {#dress-block}

- **Aliases**: dress sloper; fr: base robe
- **Family**: block (bodice plus skirt)
- **Recognize**: bodice and skirt joined at the waist with darts aligned.
- **Pieces**: bodice front/back, skirt front/back, sleeve optional
- **Shape**: waist seam at natural waist; bodice and skirt darts meet; for a dress without a waist seam,
  extend the bodice darts into the skirt (blocks.md).
- **Drafting**: 1) Use the close-fitting bodice and the straight skirt. 2) Join at the waist and check
  that dart positions line up. 3) Add length and ease at the hip as required.
- **Sewing order**: darts, side seams, shoulders, waist seam, zipper, hem
- **Finishes and notions**: zip in CB or side
- **Fabrics**: all wovens, stretch with modifications
- **Fit faults**: waist seam too short or long; fix with block lengths
- **Variants**: #close-fitting-bodice-block, #skirt-block
- **CLO3D**: bodice and skirt as separate panels sewn together
- **Confidence**: skill references.

## Jacket block {#jacket-block}

- **Aliases**: blazer block, tailored jacket block; fr: base veste
- **Family**: block (tailoring)
- **Recognize**: forepart with front dart or panel, side body, back with centre seam, two-piece sleeve.
- **Pieces**: forepart (2), side body (2), back (2), upper sleeve (2), under sleeve (2), collar (upper 1,
  under 1), facing (2)
- **Shape**: skill guide: 10 to 14 cm ease, shoulder widened by 1 cm, armhole lowered by 1.5 to 2 cm,
  two-piece sleeve (blocks.md).
- **Drafting**: 1) Start from the body block with jacket ease. 2) Widen shoulder, lower armhole. 3) Move
  the dart to the princess line or panel. 4) Draw lapel. 5) Draft two-piece sleeve.
- **Sewing order**: darts or panels, shoulders, collar, lapel, lining, sleeves
- **Finishes and notions**: canvas, interfacing, shoulder pad, lining, buttons
- **Fabrics**: wool, tweed; adjust ease for lining
- **Fit faults**: collar stand, shoulder drag; fix by shoulder slope and neckline
- **Variants**: #coat-block, #facings-and-linings-derived
- **CLO3D**: lapel roll line set by hand; interfacing layer
- **Confidence**: skill references.

## Coat block {#coat-block}

- **Aliases**: overcoat block, outerwear block; fr: base manteau
- **Family**: block (outerwear)
- **Recognize**: long length, higher ease, set to fit over jackets.
- **Pieces**: front (2), back (1 or 2), sleeve (2), collar, facings
- **Shape**: skill guide: coat bust ease 16 to 24 cm. The armhole is lowered further than for a jacket
  (amount: not verified).
- **Drafting**: 1) Add ease to the jacket block. 2) Extend length. 3) Lower the armhole. 4) Draw the
  sleeve against the new armhole.
- **Sewing order**: like a jacket, with lining and interfacing
- **Finishes and notions**: lining, buttons, interlining
- **Fabrics**: heavy wool, double faced, coating
- **Fit faults**: tightness across back under arms; add width
- **Variants**: #jacket-block
- **CLO3D**: layered setup; thick fabrics need higher density
- **Confidence**: skill references.

## Trouser block {#trouser-block}

- **Aliases**: pants block, trouser sloper; fr: base pantalon
- **Family**: block (lower body)
- **Recognize**: crotch curves (front and back), crease line, back darts.
- **Pieces**: front (2), back (2), waistband (1), pocket pieces
- **Shape**: skill drafting: widths at hip (H + ease) / 4 minus 1 front, plus 1 back; crotch extension H /
  20 front and H / 10 back; back crotch dropped 1 cm; centre back tilted 3 cm; knee 5 cm above the
  halfway point (blocks.md).
- **Drafting**: as blocks.md; Check side seams equal, back inseam slightly shorter than front.
- **Sewing order**: darts, pockets, crotch, side, inseam, waistband, fly, hem
- **Finishes and notions**: fly zipper, waistband, hook
- **Fabrics**: suiting, cotton; stretch needs less ease
- **Fit faults**: see fit-fault entries for crotch, seat, thigh
- **Variants**: #fit-crotch-adjustments, #fit-protruding-seat, #fit-flat-seat, #fit-full-thigh
- **CLO3D**: sew front/back crotch curves; check ease at hip
- **Confidence**: skill references.

## Skirt block {#skirt-block}

- **Aliases**: straight skirt block, skirt sloper; fr: base jupe
- **Family**: block (lower body)
- **Recognize**: two panels with side seams, darts at the waist, curve at the hip.
- **Pieces**: front (1 on fold), back (1 or 2)
- **Shape**: hip width (H + a) / 4 with side seam shifted 0.5 cm forward; waist-hip difference at side
  seam max 3 cm, rest in darts; darts 9 cm front, 13 cm back (blocks.md).
- **Drafting**: as blocks.md
- **Sewing order**: darts, centre back seam with zip, sides, waistband, hem
- **Finishes and notions**: zip, waistband or facing
- **Fabrics**: woven
- **Fit faults**: hip too tight, skirt riding up; fix by ease and side seam
- **Variants**: #adding-flare
- **CLO3D**: fine
- **Confidence**: skill references.

## Knit block {#knit-block}

- **Aliases**: stretch block, jersey sloper, T-shirt block; fr: base maille
- **Family**: block (stretch)
- **Recognize**: no darts, small or negative ease, straight lines.
- **Pieces**: front, back, sleeve, neck binding
- **Shape**: from the skill: remove darts, move value to side seam, sleeve cap ease zero; stretch
  classes: low up to 20 %, medium 20 to 50 %, high beyond 50 % (ease.md).
- **Drafting**: 1) Measure fabric stretch. 2) Choose negative ease. 3) Reduce width and length. 4) Test.
- **Sewing order**: shoulder, neck binding, sleeves, side seams, hem
- **Finishes and notions**: overlock, twin needle, elastic
- **Fabrics**: jersey, rib, interlock; recovery matters
- **Fit faults**: bagging at knees or elbows (recovery); fix by smaller ease
- **Variants**: #close-fitting-bodice-block
- **CLO3D**: fabric needs stretch weft/warp; calibrate with physical test
- **Confidence**: skill references.

## Children's block {#childrens-block}

- **Aliases**: kids sloper; fr: base enfant
- **Family**: block (children)
- **Recognize**: no darts, more ease, growth allowance.
- **Pieces**: bodice, sleeve, trousers, skirt
- **Shape**: from the skill: no darts, more ease, growth hem; ease default 8 cm bust, 6 cm waist (ease.md).

## Dart pivot (dart manipulation) {#dart-pivot}

- **Aliases**: dart transfer, dart rotation, dart manipulation, pivot method; fr: transfert de pince, pivotement
  de pince
- **Family**: manipulation (flat pattern)
- **Recognize**: a dart on a finished pattern that starts from a different edge than in the block (side
  seam, French dart, shoulder, armhole, neckline, centre front, waist) yet still points to the bust apex;
  the intake angle is the same as the block dart.
- **Pieces**: needs the front bodice block (half, on fold) with its apex marked and one closed dart (side
  bust dart, or waist dart as the source); produces the same piece with the dart moved. Cut count unchanged.
- **Shape**: the pivot point is the bust apex (front) or the dart point of the source dart. The new dart
  leg lines run from the new edge position to the apex. The dart angle (not the dart width at the edge)
  is what is conserved; width at the edge = angle x distance from apex, so a dart moved to a longer
  radius gets a wider mouth. The dart point is stopped short of the apex: 2 to 3 cm typical, 1 to 2 cm
  for a small bust (transformations.md). The length of the edge the dart leaves is increased by the dart
  width, so after closing, front and back side seams must still be equal (check). Source: trade
  practice taught in every flat-pattern text (Armstrong, Aldrich); no specific drawn pattern consulted,
  numeric values unverified.
- **Drafting**: 1) Trace the block, mark apex and old dart legs. 2) Draw the new dart line from the new
  edge point to the apex and slash it, stopping at the apex. 3) Pin the apex and rotate the piece so the
  old dart legs meet; the slash opens by the same angle. 4) Trace the new outline onto paper. 5) Mark
  the new dart legs, shorten them to the dart point, and true (see #truing-and-blending): fold the
  dart, trace the edge across, open the fold. 6) Notch both legs and drill 1 cm before the point.
  Partial transfer: close only a fraction (for example half the waist dart) and keep the rest.
- **Sewing order**: sew the new dart first (fold, stitch from the edge to the point, tie off or taper),
  press towards the centre (vertical darts) or downward (horizontal darts).
- **Finishes and notions**: none beyond the dart; do not stitch beyond the point.
- **Fabrics**: all wovens; thick fabrics benefit from a dart slashed open and pressed flat
- **Fit faults**: dart end bubbles: shorten the dart point set-back; dart mouth too wide after a long
  transfer: split into two darts (#slash-spread-parallel).
- **Variants**: #dart-to-gathers, #dart-to-tucks, #princess-conversion, #yoke-creation,
  #contouring-close-fit
- **CLO3D**: keep the sloper with sewn darts and move the internal lines, or close the dart in the 2D
  window and re-create it; match edge lengths with the pattern measurement tool.
- **Confidence**: method and rules: trade consensus; dart point set-back: skill transformations.md.

## Slash and spread, parallel {#slash-spread-parallel}

- **Aliases**: cut-and-spread, parallel spread, adding width; fr: ouvrir en parallele, ecarter
- **Family**: manipulation (flat pattern)
- **Recognize**: a piece wider or longer by a uniform amount along the whole slash, the original and new
  edges staying parallel: for example a body made wider at hip and hem by one even amount, or a sleeve
  lengthened at the elbow.
- **Pieces**: needs a pattern piece and 1 to n slash lines parallel to the grain line (or to each other);
  produces the same piece. Cut count unchanged.
- **Shape**: each slash goes edge to edge. The parts on each side are moved apart by the spread s,
  keeping both parts parallel to the grain line; the outline gains exactly s per slash along every
  line that crosses it. Added width = n x s. At each edge the outline is redrawn as a smooth line
  between the displaced points. Source: trade practice, no drawn pattern.
- **Drafting**: 1) Draw the slash lines on the piece. 2) Cut them fully through (or leave a 1 to 2 mm
  hinge if the piece must stay in one part). 3) Spread each by s on a sheet of paper, keeping both parts
  parallel to the grain. 4) Tape, trace the new outline, redraw edge curves. 5) Check that the sum of s
  equals the intended extra.
- **Sewing order**: not applicable to the technique; follow the garment
- **Finishes and notions**: none
- **Fabrics**: any; the extra width reads as ease in stiff fabrics and as drape in fluid ones
- **Fit faults**: bulges at the slash ends: re-curve the edge across the spread.
- **Variants**: #slash-spread-fan, #adding-pleats, #fit-full-bust-adjustment
- **CLO3D**: edit the points in the 2D window or import the new outline from DXF.
- **Confidence**: method: trade consensus; no numbers claimed.

## Slash and spread, fan {#slash-spread-fan}

- **Aliases**: pivot-and-spread, fan spread, radial flare, flare by wedge; fr: ouvrir en eventail
- **Family**: manipulation (flat pattern)
- **Recognize**: extra width appears only on one edge and the opposite edge does not change: skirt hems
  that flare from a fitted waist, sleeve hems that flare while the cap stays the same.
- **Pieces**: needs a piece and slash lines radiating from a hinge on the edge that must not change;
  produces the same piece with a curved outer edge.
- **Shape**: every slash starts at the far edge and ends 1 to 2 mm (typical) short of the hinge edge.
  Wedge angle alpha at each slash; edge gain = alpha (radians) x radius from the hinge to the edge.
  A skirt with the waist as hinge becomes a partial circle when the wedges add up to 360 degrees (full
  circle, radius r = waist / (2 pi), transformations.md). The outer edge is blended into a smooth curve
  perpendicular to each slash line. Source: trade practice.
- **Drafting**: 1) Draw the slash lines from the edge to the hinge. 2) Cut up to the hinge and rotate each
  wedge by its angle (or spread by a fixed measure at the edge). 3) Redraw the outer edge as a curve
  perpendicular to each slash line. 4) For darts, treat each dart as a closed wedge and pivot it into the
  flare (#dart-pivot with the flare option).
- **Sewing order**: follow the garment; hems on bias portions hang for 24 hours before levelling
- **Finishes and notions**: narrow hems (0.5 to 1 cm) on strongly flared edges
- **Fabrics**: fluid fabrics hang evenly; stiff fabrics stand out; bias portions stretch
- **Fit faults**: uneven ripple at the hem: re-balance the wedge angles; dipping hem: hang then level.
- **Variants**: #slash-spread-parallel, #adding-flare
- **CLO3D**: redraw the flared outline and sew; check hem edge for bias growth in the simulation.
- **Confidence**: method: trade consensus; the 1 to 2 mm hinge: trade practice.

## Dart to gathers {#dart-to-gathers}

- **Aliases**: dart converted to gathers, gathered bust, gathered waist; fr: pince transformee en fronces
- **Family**: manipulation
- **Recognize**: a gathered area at the shoulder, yoke seam, neckline or waist in place of a stitched
  dart, giving a softer finish.
- **Pieces**: needs a bodice front with the dart to convert; produces the same piece with an enlarged
  edge, plus a gathering zone marked by two notches.
- **Shape**: slash from the chosen edge to the apex, close the old dart (this opens the new slash), then
  spread two or three more parallel slashes to add gathering length. The gathering zone is the section
  of the edge between two notches; its opened edge is redrawn as a smooth convex curve. Gather ratio:
  1.5 x length thick fabric, 2 x medium, 2.5 to 3 x fine (transformations.md). The gathered edge is
  sewn to the straight edge of a yoke, band or facing of the length of the opposing piece. Source: trade
  practice; ratios from the skill's transformations.md.
- **Drafting**: 1) Choose the edge and the zone length. 2) Slash and open as in #dart-pivot. 3) Add
  extra spread by additional slashes to reach the ratio. 4) Mark the zone with notches. 5) Redraw the
  edge as a smooth curve.
- **Sewing order**: two rows of long stitches in the zone, gather to the length of the opposing piece,
  sew, finish the seam together.
- **Finishes and notions**: stay tape, elastic thread for shirring
- **Fabrics**: fine to medium wovens; thick fabrics bulk
- **Fit faults**: gathers sit off the apex: re-centre the zone; too much volume: lower the ratio.
- **Variants**: #dart-pivot, #dart-to-tucks, #adding-flare
- **CLO3D**: use the shirring/gather setting on the seam; ratio is the seam length difference.
- **Confidence**: method and ratios: trade practice (skill references).

## Dart to tucks {#dart-to-tucks}

- **Aliases**: dart to pin tucks, release tucks; fr: pince transformee en plis, nervures
- **Family**: manipulation
- **Recognize**: a row of narrow folds, stitched down for a few centimetres then released, on a yoke or
  over the bust, taking the place of a dart.
- **Pieces**: needs a front bodice or a yoke; produces the same piece with extra width at the tuck edge.
- **Shape**: replace the dart by n parallel slashes (typical 3 to 5), each spread by (dart intake) / n;
  each tuck is a fold that runs towards the apex but stops well short of it. Fold lines are drawn parallel
  to the grain; the folded-away part is shown on the pattern as lines with an arrow for fold direction.
  Tuck stitching length: not known, design dependent. Source: trade practice, unverified.
- **Drafting**: 1) Divide the dart intake by n. 2) Draw slashes from the edge towards the apex. 3) Spread
  each by its share. 4) Mark fold lines and direction (towards the centre). 5) Mark where stitching
  stops.
- **Sewing order**: mark fold lines, fold, stitch the tucks before assembling seams; press direction
  fixed.
- **Finishes and notions**: lightweight interfacing optional
- **Fabrics**: crisp cotton, silk, poplin
- **Fit faults**: tucks pull at the bust: end them earlier or spread them differently.
- **Variants**: #dart-to-gathers, #adding-pleats
- **CLO3D**: model with internal lines and a strong fold angle, or shrink the seam; test visually.
- **Confidence**: method: trade practice; tuck length: not known.

## Princess conversion {#princess-conversion}

- **Aliases**: princess line, princess seam, shoulder princess, armhole princess; fr: couture princesse
- **Family**: manipulation (style line)
- **Recognize**: seams from the shoulder (or armhole) over the bust to the hem, dividing the front into a
  centre panel and a side panel. Often a similar pair of seams on the back.
- **Pieces**: front centre panel (2, left and right), front side panel (2), back centre panel (2, or 1 on
  fold when the back has no seam), back side panel (2).
- **Shape**: the line starts at the shoulder (about halfway between neck point and shoulder tip, typical)
  or at the armhole, passes through or very close to the bust apex, continues through the waist dart and
  ends at the hem. Bust dart and waist dart are both closed into this line so each seam holds the dart
  value. The centre panel edge is concave over the bust, the side panel edge is convex (the bust curve
  lies in the side panel); the convex edge is sewn to the concave edge and the concave allowance is
  clipped. Notch both pieces at bust level and at waist. Check: lengths along the seam match within the
  ease at the apex (transformations.md). Source: skill transformations.md and common teaching; start
  points unverified.
- **Drafting**: 1) Draw the line apex to shoulder and apex to waist dart, as one smooth curve. 2) Close the
  bust dart by #dart-pivot into the line. 3) Cut along the line and separate. 4) Share the waist dart
  between the two seam edges. 5) Redraw both edges (#truing-and-blending). 6) Add notches.
- **Sewing order**: sew centre to side on each side, clip the concave allowance at the bust, press open or
  towards the centre.
- **Finishes and notions**: clipped seam, boning channel on the seam optional
- **Fabrics**: all wovens; helpful on stiff cloth as it avoids dart bulk
- **Fit faults**: point at the bust: move the line 1 to 2 cm to the side of the apex (typical); stretched
  seam: stay-tape the convex edge.
- **Variants**: #dart-pivot, #yoke-creation, #contouring-close-fit
- **CLO3D**: sew convex edge to concave edge; use a finer mesh at the bust.
- **Confidence**: method: trade practice; start positions: unverified.

## Yoke creation {#yoke-creation}

- **Aliases**: yoke, shoulder yoke, hip yoke; fr: empiecement, passe
- **Family**: manipulation (style line)
- **Recognize**: a horizontal or curved seam across the back of a shirt, across the chest on a front, or
  across the hip of a skirt or trouser, separating a close-fitting top part from a lower part that is
  gathered, pleated or flared.
- **Pieces**: shirt back yoke (2, or 1 on fold), back body (1 on fold), front yoke if any (2), front
  body (2); skirt hip yoke front and back (1 each, 2 for a pair), lower skirt front and back.
- **Shape**: the shirt back yoke line runs from armhole to centre back, roughly horizontal (blocks.md:
  yoke cut from a horizontal line); its height is a design choice and unverified. The shoulder dart
  value is closed into the yoke line so the yoke sits flat. The upper edge of the lower body piece
  gets the opened-out value as ease, to be gathered or pleated; the edges sewn together are the lower
  edge of the yoke and the upper edge of the body and must be equal after the gathers or pleats. A skirt
  hip yoke closes the waist dart into the yoke line and moves the value into a flare below. Source: skill
  transformations.md and common teaching.
- **Drafting**: 1) Draw the yoke line. 2) Pivot any dart into the yoke line (#dart-pivot). 3) Cut the
  yoke off. 4) Add gather or pleat allowance to the lower piece by #slash-spread-parallel. 5) Notch
  centre and quarter points.
- **Sewing order**: yoke to body (shirts are typically assembled with the two yokes enclosing the body
  edge, then flipped), then shoulders and sides.
- **Finishes and notions**: double yoke on shirts (inner yoke), topstitching 2 mm from the seam
- **Fabrics**: all wovens; checks and stripes need matching at the yoke seam
- **Fit faults**: pull lines at the shoulder blades: raise the yoke line or add a yoke-line dart.
- **Variants**: #dart-to-gathers, #adding-pleats, #shirt-block
- **CLO3D**: sew yoke to body; use shirring on the body edge if gathered.
- **Confidence**: method: trade practice; heights: not known.

## Adding flare {#adding-flare}

- **Aliases**: A-line, flaring a skirt, flare by dart pivot; fr: evasement, ajouter de l'ampleur
- **Family**: manipulation
- **Recognize**: skirts, sleeves, peplums and dresses with a hem wider than the waist, from a mild A-line
  to a full circle.
- **Pieces**: needs the skirt block (front, back); produces front and back with the hem widened.
- **Shape**: waist unchanged; side seams angled outwards. A-line: darts closed and their value moved to
  the hem (transformations.md: pivot each dart with the flare option, then smooth the hem). Stronger
  flare: fan slash (#slash-spread-fan). Circle skirt: waist radius = waist / (2 pi) full circle, waist /
  pi half circle, 2 x waist / pi quarter circle, hem radius = r + length (transformations.md). The hem
  is curved perpendicular to each slash line. Source: skill transformations.md.
- **Drafting**: as #slash-spread-fan; for an A-line, close each dart by pivot; for circle skirts, draw the
  arcs directly.
- **Sewing order**: darts (if any), side seams, waistband, hang, hem
- **Finishes and notions**: narrow hem (0.5 to 1 cm) when the flare is strong
- **Fabrics**: fluid crepe falls evenly; stiff cotton stands out; bias portions grow
- **Fit faults**: uneven hem: let the skirt hang 24 hours before levelling; uneven ripple: re-balance
  wedges.
- **Variants**: #slash-spread-fan, #skirt-block, #bias-drape
- **CLO3D**: align panels to grain; hem growth shows in the simulation.
- **Confidence**: skill references; circle formulas follow from geometry.

## Adding pleats {#adding-pleats}

- **Aliases**: knife pleat, box pleat, inverted pleat, pleat allowance; fr: ajouter des plis
- **Family**: manipulation
- **Recognize**: folded vertical pleats at waist or hem; pressed all the way down or stitched down from the
  waist and released.
- **Pieces**: needs the skirt or sleeve block; produces the same piece with extra width at each pleat.
- **Shape**: each pleat adds twice its depth for a knife pleat (transformations.md); a box or inverted
  pleat is two opposed folds and adds four times the fold depth (derived from the same rule). Pleat
  lines: fold line and placement line, arrows for fold direction. Grain line stays parallel to the pleat.
  Source: skill transformations.md; individual designs unverified.
- **Drafting**: 1) Mark pleat positions on the block. 2) Slash and spread by 2 x depth (knife) or 4 x
  depth (box). 3) Mark fold and placement lines, direction, and stitching stop. 4) The folded waist width
  must equal the waistband length.
- **Sewing order**: press pleats, tack at the waist, stitch down to the stop, finish the hem
- **Finishes and notions**: pleat underlay lining optional; topstitching for a pressed look
- **Fabrics**: wool and polyester hold pleats; crisp cotton
- **Fit faults**: pleats open at the hip: stitch further down.
- **Variants**: #slash-spread-parallel, #dart-to-tucks
- **CLO3D**: use fold or pleat tools, or model the folded geometry in the 2D patterns.
- **Confidence**: knife rule: skill references; box rule: derived, trade practice.

## Cowl development {#cowl-development}

- **Aliases**: cowl neckline, draped neck, cowl front; fr: col bénitier, col drape
- **Family**: manipulation
- **Recognize**: soft folds falling from the neckline across the chest (or the back).
- **Pieces**: needs a front bodice with the bust dart at the neckline or side; produces a bodice front
  wider at the neckline. Cut 1 on fold, often on the bias.
- **Shape**: slash several lines (3 to 4, typical) from the neckline edge to the apex (or to the side
  seam) and spread them at the neckline; the neckline edge lengthens and becomes the cowl edge. Cowl
  depth is controlled by the spread per slash: more spread, deeper fall. Spread amounts for a given
  depth: not known. The neckline curve is redrawn as a smooth line; the shoulder usually gets a small
  dart or ease. The cowl edge is faced or lined with the same shape. Source: unverified, trade practice.
- **Drafting**: 1) Draw slash lines from neckline to apex. 2) Spread each. 3) Blend the neckline.
  4) Test in muslin on the grain and on the bias; adjust.
- **Sewing order**: shoulder darts or tucks, facing or lining joined at the neckline, shoulders, sides.
- **Finishes and notions**: lining or self-facing; cowl weights optional
- **Fabrics**: jersey, crepe, satin; the fall depends on fabric and grain
- **Fit faults**: cowl too shallow: add spread; cowl gapes at the armhole: add shoulder dart.
- **Variants**: #cowl-drape, #bias-drape
- **CLO3D**: simulate with a fluid fabric; judge the fall visually.
- **Confidence**: method: trade practice; amounts: not known.

## Contouring (close-fit shaping) {#contouring-close-fit}

- **Aliases**: contoured patterns, close-fit shaping, bustier shaping; fr: sculpter le patron
- **Family**: manipulation
- **Recognize**: close-fitting patterns that follow the body: strapless, bustier, corset-like tops, fitted
  princess bodices. No ease at bust and waist; style lines hug the body.
- **Pieces**: needs the bodice block; produces panels with style lines (each panel cut 2, left and
  right).
- **Shape**: ease is reduced to zero or negative; bust and waist darts are moved into seams through the
  apex; the waist is nipped; the top edge and the underarm follow the body (raised at the side, lower at
  the centre). Source: Armstrong treats contouring as a chapter; details unverified.
- **Drafting**: 1) Trace the block and remove ease at the side seams. 2) Put the darts into seams
  (#princess-conversion). 3) Shape the top edge. 4) Mark boning positions and hem. 5) Test on an avatar
  or a form.
- **Sewing order**: panels, boning channels, lining, closure
- **Finishes and notions**: boning, waist stay, zip or hook tape
- **Fabrics**: stable wovens, coutil, interlined satin
- **Fit faults**: gaping at the top edge: shorten the top edge by a wedge; slipping down: add length at the
  side.
- **Variants**: #princess-conversion, #close-fitting-bodice-block
- **CLO3D**: set fabric stiffness; avatar fit test with a tension map.
- **Confidence**: method: trade practice; the chapter detail in Armstrong: not verified.

## Raglan from set-in sleeve {#raglan-from-set-in}

- **Aliases**: raglan sleeve, converting a set-in sleeve to raglan; fr: manche raglan
- **Family**: manipulation (sleeve)
- **Recognize**: seams from the neckline to the underarm and no shoulder tip; either a one-piece sleeve
  with a shoulder dart, or a two-piece sleeve with a shoulder seam inside the sleeve.
- **Pieces**: front (1 on fold or 2), back (1 on fold or 2), sleeve front (2), sleeve back (2); or one
  sleeve with a shoulder dart and an underarm seam.
- **Shape**: the raglan line starts on the neckline of front and back, crosses the shoulder area and ends
  at the underarm on the armhole (transformations.md). The part of the bodice cut off by the line is
  joined to the sleeve cap. Seam lengths of bodice side and sleeve side must be equal. The one-piece
  type has a shoulder dart on the sleeve which takes up the shoulder slope. Exact start and end points
  vary with the design and are unverified. Source: skill transformations.md; teaching practice.
- **Drafting**: 1) Draw the raglan line on front and back blocks. 2) Cut it off. 3) Place the sleeve with
  the shoulder point aligned, and join the cut-off parts to the sleeve cap along the matching edges. 4)
  Close the shoulder if one-piece and add a dart. 5) Check seam lengths.
- **Sewing order**: sleeve pieces to front and back along the raglan seams, then underarm and side seam in
  one pass.
- **Finishes and notions**: clip curve at the underarm; knit raglans use overlock
- **Fabrics**: knits are common; wovens need the dart or seam
- **Fit faults**: drag at the shoulder: lower the raglan line or add the dart.
- **Variants**: #kimono-from-block-gusset, #dropped-shoulder
- **CLO3D**: sew the sleeve pieces to the body; check seam lengths in the 2D window.
- **Confidence**: method: trade practice; positions: unverified.

- **Aliases**: kimono sleeve with underarm gusset, diamond gusset; fr: manche kimono avec carre d'aisance
- **Family**: manipulation (sleeve)
- **Recognize**: body and sleeve in one piece, with a diamond or triangle inserted in the underarm slit to

- **Pieces**: front (1), back (1), gusset (1 square, or 2 triangles) per underarm.
- **Shape**: the underarm seam is slit from the body edge into the sleeve. A square gusset is set on the

- **Finishes and notions**: reinforcement of the slit end
- **Fabrics**: wovens; a bias gusset moves with the arm
- **Fit faults**: pulling at the gusset corner: deepen the slit; wrinkles: lower the underarm.

## Dropped shoulder {#dropped-shoulder}

- **Aliases**: drop shoulder, extended shoulder, oversized shoulder; fr: epaule tombante
- **Family**: manipulation (sleeve and armhole)
- **Recognize**: the shoulder seam ends well past the natural shoulder point on the upper arm; deep, shallow
  armhole; flat sleeve cap.
- **Pieces**: front (1), back (1), sleeve (2), a flat-cap sleeve.
- **Shape**: the shoulder point is moved outwards and down along the shoulder line; the underarm point is
  lowered; the armhole becomes a shallow curve; the sleeve cap is lower and the sleeve wider; cap ease is

- **Fabrics**: knit, brushed fabrics, sweatshirt fleece
- **Fit faults**: arm lift restricted: lower the underarm more or widen the sleeve.

## Facings and linings derived {#facings-and-linings-derived}

- **Family**: manipulation (finishing patterns)
- **Recognize**: the inside finishing pieces that follow a garment edge.
- **Pieces**: front facing (2), back neck facing (1), armhole facing (2), lining front (2), lining back

- **Finishes and notions**: interfacing on facings, understitching, lining hanging free at the hem

## Truing and blending {#truing-and-blending}

- **Aliases**: truing seams, blending, squaring off, walking the pattern; fr: verifier les lignes, raccorder
- **Family**: manipulation (checking)
- **Recognize**: last stage of a flat pattern: paired edges are compared, corners smoothed, darts folded to
  check the seam lines.
- **Pieces**: any pattern piece before seam allowances; cut count unchanged.
- **Shape**: paired edges have equal length except for planned ease; edges that meet when joined (shoulder
  at neck and armhole, waist at side seams, hem at side seams, crotch at inseam) continue without an
  angle; the hem meets the side seam at a right angle; darts are folded and the edge traced through the
  fold (finishes.md). Source: skill finishes.md.
- **Drafting**: 1) Measure paired edges and write the lengths. 2) Fix mismatches where least visible. 3)
  Pin or fold the pieces at the seam and redraw curves smoothly across. 4) Fold each dart and trace the
  edge through it. 5) Add notches and, last, the seam allowances.
- **Sewing order**: not applicable
- **Finishes and notions**: French curve, hip curve, notcher
- **Fabrics**: not applicable

## Preparing the stand (dress form and muslin) {#preparing-the-stand}

- **Aliases**: dress form preparation, padding the form, style taping, muslin preparation; fr: preparer le
  mannequin, mise en toile, marquage du buste
- **Family**: draping
- **Recognize**: a dress form with tape lines on the body (centre front, centre back, bust level, waist,
  hip, princess lines, neckline and armhole) and muslin pieces with ruled grain lines.
- **Pieces**: dress form (1), muslin rectangles per piece to drape (bodice front, bodice back, skirt front,
  skirt back, sleeve), style tape, pins.
- **Shape**: form: pad or adjust it to the client's measurements; mark with tape CF and CB (vertical), bust
  line, waist line and hip line (horizontal, level to the floor), side seam, neck base and armhole.
  Muslin rectangles: cut larger than the piece by an excess on all sides (about 5 cm typical, not
  verified for each text) so the edge can be pinned and trimmed. Mark lengthwise grain at CF or CB and
  crosswise grain at bust and hip. Fabric is torn or pulled straight, pressed, and its grain is true
  (squared) before marking. Source: general draping practice (Joseph-Armstrong's draping text uses a
  common grid; see #joseph-armstrong); numeric excess unverified.
- **Drafting**: 1) Fix the form to its height and check it is level. 2) Apply tape lines. 3) Straighten
  and press the muslin; draw lengthwise and crosswise grain lines. 4) Mark the key levels (bust, waist,
  hip) on the muslin so they can be matched to the form.
- **Sewing order**: not applicable
- **Finishes and notions**: style tape, T-pins, fine pencil or pen, tracing wheel, curve ruler
- **Fabrics**: muslin of a weight similar to the final fabric; fluid fabrics are first draped in muslin or in
  the final fabric itself
- **Fit faults**: a form that does not match the client: pad it before draping; fitting a drape on a form
  of a different size gives a drape that does not fit the client.
- **Variants**: #bodice-drape, #skirt-drape
- **CLO3D**: the avatar replaces the form; set pose and measurements before placing patterns.
- **Confidence**: practice: trade practice; the 5 cm excess: typical, not verified.

## Bodice drape {#bodice-drape}

- **Aliases**: draped bodice, draped sloper, front and back bodice drape; fr: moulage du corsage
- **Family**: draping
- **Recognize**: a muslin pinned to the form, with darts and seams marked directly from the body.
- **Pieces**: bodice front (1 half), bodice back (1 half), mirror for the other side.
- **Shape**: the front muslin has CF on the lengthwise grain along the form's CF and the crosswise grain
  on the bust line; the muslin is smoothed over the bust and the side, with fullness pinned out as a
  dart (side, French or waist) rather than ironed away. Back muslin: CB on lengthwise grain, crosswise
  grain at the shoulder blades; ease at the shoulder blade is pinned as a shoulder dart or ease.
  Neckline, shoulder, armhole, side seams and waist are marked with pins and then drawn. Source: general
  draping practice (#joseph-armstrong); unverified for specific figures.
- **Drafting**: 1) Pin the muslin CF at neck and waist, bust level horizontal. 2) Smooth to the side,
  pin at the shoulder and the underarm. 3) Pin out the dart in the chosen position. 4) Mark neckline
  (clip to the line), shoulder, armhole and side seam. 5) Do the back. 6) Mark waist. 7) Remove and true
  (see #transferring-to-paper).
- **Sewing order**: not applicable
- **Finishes and notions**: pins, pencil
- **Fabrics**: muslin; fluid fabric drapes differently, so confirm on the final cloth where relevant
- **Fit faults**: crosswise grain off horizontal at the bust: re-pin; strain at the underarm: leave more
  ease at the side.
- **Variants**: #close-fitting-bodice-block, #cowl-drape, #bias-drape

- **Confidence**: practice: trade practice; no numbers claimed.

## Skirt drape {#skirt-drape}

- **Aliases**: draped skirt, skirt sloper drape; fr: moulage de la jupe
- **Family**: draping
- **Recognize**: muslin on the hips with darts at the waist and side seams marked from the form.

## Cowl drape {#cowl-drape}

- **Aliases**: draped cowl, cowl neckline on the stand; fr: moulage du col drape
- **Family**: draping
- **Recognize**: soft folds falling from a high neck or shoulder point, produced by pinning the fabric at
  the shoulders and letting the neckline hang.
- **Pieces**: a muslin rectangle larger than the bodice front (cut 1 on fold, on the bias or on grain).
- **Shape**: the fabric hangs from two shoulder pin points at the neck; the cowl depth is set by how much

  2) Smooth the sides to the waist and pin. 3) Mark shoulder, armhole, side and waist; mark the cowl

  with the flat method of #cowl-development.

- **Finishes and notions**: pins
- **Fabrics**: jersey, crepe, charmeuse; stiffer fabrics stand away
- **Fit faults**: cowl too shallow or flat: release more fabric at the neckline; cowl gapes at the armhole:
  pin a small dart at the shoulder.

## Bias drape {#bias-drape}

- **Aliases**: bias-cut draping, bias dress drape; fr: moulage en biais
- **Family**: draping
- **Recognize**: dresses and skirts where the fabric is draped at 45 degrees to the grain, giving a
  body-skimming fall.
- **Pieces**: bias-cut muslin or fabric pieces (front, back), larger than the finished garment.
- **Shape**: the garment centre line follows the bias (45 degrees to warp and weft); the weave stretches
  when pulled and shortens in width. Pieces are draped without pulling, then allowed to hang before
  trimming; the hem is levelled after hanging (24 hours typical, transformations.md). Source: skill
  transformations.md (bias note); the stretch percentage: not known.
- **Drafting**: 1) Mark the bias on the muslin with a 45 degree square. 2) Pin to the form without
  stretching. 3) Smooth, pin seams at the side. 4) Mark seams lightly. 5) Let it hang and re-mark.
- **Sewing order**: sew seams with the pieces unstretched; hang before the hem
- **Finishes and notions**: narrow hem (rolled or machine)
- **Fabrics**: silk charmeuse, crepe, satin; plain-weave cotton
- **Fit faults**: hem dips at side seams: level after hanging; waist stretches: stay-tape.
- **Variants**: #adding-flare, #bias-cutting
- **CLO3D**: set the grain at 45 degrees; allow the sim to stretch the cloth.
- **Confidence**: practice: trade practice.

## Transferring a drape to paper {#transferring-to-paper}

- **Aliases**: truing a drape, drape to flat pattern, marking and trueing; fr: reporter le moulage sur papier
- **Family**: draping
- **Recognize**: the final stage of draping: the marked muslin is pressed and drawn out on paper.
- **Pieces**: draped muslin pieces (front, back, skirt, sleeve); output: paper pattern pieces.
- **Shape**: the muslin is removed, pressed flat, and dart legs, seam lines and notches are measured and
  drawn as straight lines or smooth curves. Dart legs are made equal. Seam allowances are added
  last. Seam lengths of adjoining pieces are compared (#truing-and-blending). Source: general draping
  practice.
- **Drafting**: 1) Remove and press the muslin. 2) Lay it on paper and trace the marks with a tracing wheel,
  or pierce through with a pin. 3) Connect the dots with ruler and curve. 4) Fold darts and true the edge
  across them. 5) Compare adjoining seams. 6) Sew a second muslin from the paper pattern and check.
- **Sewing order**: not applicable
- **Finishes and notions**: tracing wheel, paper, French curve, notcher
- **Fabrics**: muslin
- **Fit faults**: lines transferred off-grain give a twisted pattern: keep grain lines on the muslin and
  transfer them.
- **Variants**: #truing-and-blending, #toile-process
- **CLO3D**: export the final flat shapes as DXF to import.
- **Confidence**: practice: trade practice.

## Toile process {#toile-process}

- **Aliases**: muslin fitting, test garment, mock-up, prototype fitting; fr: toile, essayage de la toile
- **Family**: fitting
- **Recognize**: a test garment made in cheap cloth of similar weight to the final fabric, fitted on the
  body or form, marked and unpicked to correct the paper pattern.
- **Pieces**: all pieces of the pattern, with seam allowance and balance lines; the final fabric's
  grain, not the cheapest cloth's.
- **Shape**: pieces are cut from fabric with CF/CB, bust, waist and hip balance lines drawn on the
  cloth so the fitter can see whether the lines stay horizontal and vertical on the body. Seams are
  machine-basted at the real seam allowance (1 cm typical, finishes.md); the toile has no facing. Source:
  trade practice.
- **Drafting**: 1) Cut pieces on grain and mark balance lines. 2) Baste. 3) Fit on a person in this
  order: balance (CF/CB vertical, bust and hip lines horizontal), length, circumferences, shoulder, neck,
  armhole, sleeve. 4) Mark each correction with pins and pen. 5) Unpick, flatten and copy to the paper
  pattern (#transferring-to-paper). 6) Make a second toile if the changes were large.
- **Sewing order**: baste in the order of the final sewing; leave hem and closures pinned
- **Finishes and notions**: calico or muslin, tape for balance lines, tracing wheel
- **Fabrics**: toile cloth with similar drape; for stretch fabrics, use the intended knit, not a woven
- **Fit faults**: see the fit-fault entries (#fit-gaping-neckline and following)
- **Variants**: #bodice-drape, #fit-full-bust-adjustment
- **CLO3D**: a 3D simulation can replace the first toile but not the final check on the body.
- **Confidence**: practice: trade practice.

## Fit fault: gaping neckline {#fit-gaping-neckline}

- **Aliases**: neckline gapes, standing neckline, neck edge stands away; fr: encolure baillante, col qui baille
- **Family**: fitting (neckline, bodice front)
- **Recognize**: the front neckline lifts away from the chest, forming a gap, most visible in V and
  scoop necks and in bias or stretched edges.
- **Pieces**: bodice front and facing; the facing is redrawn after the fix.
- **Shape**: the neckline edge is too long for the chest wall (hollow chest, narrow upper chest, stretched
  bias). The excess is removed from the edge, transferred into a dart or tuck, or into the shoulder.
  Source: trade practice; the amounts depend on the gap measured at the toile and are not known.
- **Drafting**: 1) Pin out the gap on the toile and measure it. 2) On the pattern, draw a line from the
  middle of the neckline to the apex. 3) Cut and close at the neckline by the measured gap, which opens
  the same value at the bust dart (#dart-pivot). 4) Redraw the neckline across the closed gap and the
  facing to match. 5) Alternatives: reduce the neckline width at the shoulder, or make the edge on the
  straight grain with stay tape.
- **Sewing order**: stay-tape the neckline edge before facing
- **Finishes and notions**: stay tape, interfaced facing
- **Fabrics**: bias or loose-weave fabrics gape more than stable ones
- **Fit faults**: this is the fault entry
- **Variants**: #fit-armhole-gape, #fit-small-bust-adjustment
- **CLO3D**: check on the avatar with a stretch map; stay tape can be added as a seam tape.
- **Confidence**: method: trade practice; no amounts.

## Fit fault: armhole gape {#fit-armhole-gape}

## Fit fault: forward shoulder {#fit-forward-shoulder}

## Fit fault: full bust adjustment {#fit-full-bust-adjustment}

- **Aliases**: FBA, large bust adjustment; fr: poitrine forte, ajustement pour forte poitrine
- **Family**: fitting (bust)
- **Recognize**: pulling from the side seam to the bust, buttons or front opening gape, side seam draws
  forward, the front hem rides up, dart too short for the bust.
- **Pieces**: bodice front (half), and facing and sleeve checks.
- **Shape**: the front is too narrow and too short over the bust. Extra width is added at the apex area and
  extra length at the side seam; the dart increases. Needed addition per half front = the extra bust
  circumference needed divided by 4 (the front has two halves and the back two, all of the correction
  goes in the front half). Source: trade practice, widely taught; typical cup-size steps: not
  verified.
- **Drafting**: 1) Mark the apex. 2) Draw a vertical line from mid-shoulder (or armhole) through the apex
  to the hem, and a horizontal line from the apex to the side seam. 3) Cut along both lines to the apex.
  4) Spread the vertical cut by the amount (extra circumference / 4) and lower the side piece by the
  same value to open the horizontal cut. 5) Redraw the dart: its width equals the new vertical gain;
  redistribute the waist dart. 6) Redraw the side seam, shoulder and hem. 7) Re-check the sleeve cap
  length and the facing.
- **Sewing order**: unchanged, with the larger dart
- **Finishes and notions**: none
- **Fabrics**: wovens; stretch fabrics need less
- **Fit faults**: this is the fault entry
- **Variants**: #fit-small-bust-adjustment, #slash-spread-parallel, #dart-pivot
- **CLO3D**: edit the points or re-import the DXF.
- **Confidence**: division by 4: verified by geometry; cut lines: trade practice.

## Fit fault: crotch adjustments {#fit-crotch-adjustments}

- **Aliases**: crotch depth, crotch length, rise adjustment, whiskers, camel toe; fr: fourche, hauteur de fourche
- **Family**: fitting (trouser)
- **Recognize**: a rise too short shows diagonal drag lines from the crotch outward and a tight crotch; a rise
  too long shows loose folds below the crotch and a waist that hangs low; a crotch curve too short or
  long shows pull lines forward or backward.
- **Pieces**: trouser front and back, waistband.
- **Shape**: two separate quantities are changed: the crotch depth (vertical distance, waist to crotch
  point) and the crotch length (curve length, front plus back). Depth: add or remove length above
  the crotch level, horizontally across the piece (hip level), in front and back equally. Length: move
  the crotch hook (the extension) out or in, and redraw the curves. Source: trade practice; amounts
  measured on the toile and not known.
- **Drafting**: 1) Measure the depth or length error on the toile. 2) Depth: cut across the piece
  between the waist and the crotch line and spread or overlap by the amount. 3) Length: extend or
  shorten the hook by the amount, adding half in the front and half in the back, and redraw the
  curves. 4) Check front and back crotch curves are about the same length (back slightly longer, per the
  block's checks in blocks.md). 5) Check the waistband.
- **Sewing order**: unchanged
- **Finishes and notions**: none
- **Fabrics**: all
- **Fit faults**: this is the fault entry
- **Variants**: #trouser-block, #fit-protruding-seat, #fit-flat-seat, #fit-full-thigh
- **CLO3D**: sew the crotch and check the tension maps on the avatar.
- **Confidence**: method: trade practice.

## Fit fault: sleeve twist {#fit-sleeve-twist}

- **Aliases**: twisting sleeve, sleeve swings forward or back, rotated sleeve; fr: manche qui vrille
- **Family**: fitting (sleeve)
- **Recognize**: diagonal wrinkles running from the cap around the arm, the sleeve seam moves forward or
  backward from the arm's side, the cuff does not hang straight.
- **Pieces**: sleeve (and armhole of the bodice).
- **Shape**: the cap is mis-rotated relative to the arm. Move the sleeve's notches (shoulder point, front
  and back notch) by a small amount along the armhole, in the direction that straightens the seam; the
  sleeve cap is reshaped so the front cap and back cap lengths keep their ease. Source: trade practice;
  amounts measured on the toile and not known.
- **Drafting**: 1) Mark on the toile the amount the sleeve must rotate. 2) Move the shoulder notch on the
  armhole by that amount. 3) Re-draw the cap to match the armhole, keeping the total cap ease. 4)
  Check the grain line: it must hang vertical. 5) Check the sleeve length at the front and back.
- **Sewing order**: set the sleeve in with the shifted notches
- **Finishes and notions**: none
- **Fabrics**: all
- **Fit faults**: this is the fault entry
- **Variants**: #fit-forward-shoulder, #dropped-shoulder
- **CLO3D**: align the sleeve notches to the shoulder point and check the sleeve hang with the avatar's arm
  pose.
- **Confidence**: method: trade practice.

## Grade rules {#grade-rules}

- **Aliases**: grade rule table, grade increments, rule library, grade specs; fr: regles de gradation, table de gradation
- **Family**: grading
- **Recognize**: a table of x/y displacements per size step attached to each point of each pattern piece.
- **Pieces**: needs a base size pattern with points defined; produces every other size of the same piece.
- **Shape**: a grade rule is the amount a pattern is enlarged or reduced at a given point (the cardinal
  point) for one size step, as a vector (dx, dy) relative to a fixed reference point of the piece. Points
  near the reference move little, points at the side and the underarm move most. The rule applies from the
  base size in both directions (positive to larger sizes, negative to smaller). Grade rules can change
  with the size range (for example a bust step of one size 6 to 8, larger beyond size 12, per one grading
  teacher's example). Source: general grading practice; no drawn pattern.
- **Drafting**: 1) Choose the base size (usually a middle size, size 10 or 12 in US practice, not the
  smallest). 2) Choose the reference point of each piece (CF at the waist or at a similar fixed
  point). 3) List the cardinal points. 4) For each point set dx, dy per size step so that the sum of
  rule steps along each measurement matches the size chart's step (for example bust step / 4 for a
  half-front piece). 5) Keep the piece shape: check neckline width and armhole depth steps. 6) Check
  every seam pair in the graded size (#truing-and-blending).
- **Sewing order**: not applicable
- **Finishes and notions**: rule table in the pattern CAD, or a spreadsheet
- **Fabrics**: not applicable
- **Fit faults**: a grade that keeps the proportions of the base size does not fit if the figure type
  changes; check at least one large and one small size on a person or avatar.
- **Variants**: #cardinal-points, #nest-grading, #proportional-grading, #size-charts
- **CLO3D**: use CLO grading (size editing) or import graded DXF; verify size by size.
- **Confidence**: concepts: verified (fashion-incubator, teaching notes); step values: not given.

## Nest grading {#nest-grading}

- **Aliases**: nested sizes, nested pattern, size nest; fr: patron gradue superpose
- **Family**: grading
- **Recognize**: all sizes of a piece overlaid on one outline, with the reference point common and the larger
  and smaller outlines around the base.
- **Pieces**: the same piece in every size, drawn on one sheet.
- **Shape**: every size outline is the base outline displaced by the grade rule vectors times the number of
  steps. The outlines share the reference point (CF at waist, for example) and fan out from it;
  corners and curves are re-drawn per size, with the same relationship to the point. The nest is
  used to check that curves stay smooth in all sizes and that seam lengths progress evenly. Source:
  trade practice; the skill overlays outlines in pattern.svg for sizes (finishes.md).
- **Drafting**: 1) Plot every size. 2) Check that the outline offsets are even between sizes. 3) Check
  neckline, armhole and hem curves for kinks. 4) Fix the rule at any point that jumps.
- **Sewing order**: not applicable
- **Finishes and notions**: coloured lines per size on paper or screen
- **Fabrics**: not applicable
- **Fit faults**: uneven spacing of the outlines indicates a mistake in a rule.
- **Variants**: #grade-rules, #proportional-grading
- **CLO3D**: change size in the grading panel to view each size.
- **Confidence**: concept: trade practice.

## Proportional grading {#proportional-grading}

- **Aliases**: measurement-based grading, redrafting per size, scaling; fr: gradation proportionnelle
- **Family**: grading
- **Recognize**: a method in which a size is obtained by redrafting from that size's measurements (or by
  proportional scaling) instead of moving points by fixed vectors.
- **Pieces**: the same piece in each size.
- **Shape**: two forms exist. (1) Redraft: use the size chart's measurements and the block formulas to draft
  each size (the skill's `--sizes` command, finishes.md, keeps each size's proportions). (2) Proportional
  scaling: points are moved by a fraction of the measurement difference (for example width points by
  bust step / 4). Pure scaling of the whole piece is rarely correct, because not all body measurements
  grow in proportion (for instance neck and shoulder grow less than bust). Beyond three or four sizes
  apart, draft a new block rather than grade (finishes.md). Source: skill finishes.md.
- **Drafting**: 1) Take the size chart. 2) Redraft each size or compute the fraction for each point. 3) Compare
  the outlines in a nest (#nest-grading). 4) Check seams.
- **Sewing order**: not applicable
- **Finishes and notions**: not applicable
- **Fabrics**: not applicable
- **Fit faults**: scaling the whole pattern gives wrong shoulder and neck width.
- **Variants**: #grade-rules, #size-charts
- **CLO3D**: avatar sizes can be proportionally scaled; check the garment on each avatar.
- **Confidence**: skill references; concept: trade practice.

## Cardinal points {#cardinal-points}

- **Aliases**: grade points, grading points, key points; fr: points de gradation
- **Family**: grading
- **Recognize**: the specific points on a piece where a grade rule is applied: corners and key landmarks.
- **Pieces**: all pattern pieces.
- **Shape**: typical cardinal points on a bodice: neck point at the shoulder, shoulder tip, armhole notch
  (halfway down the armhole), underarm point, side seam at the waist, side seam at the hem, centre front
  and centre back at the waist and the hem. Points at the centre have small steps, points at the side and
  the shoulder have large steps. Between cardinal points the curves are redrawn. Source: grading
  teaching sources (fashion-incubator, course notes); lists vary by company.
- **Drafting**: 1) Mark cardinal points on the base pattern. 2) Number them. 3) Assign a rule to each.
  4) For curves, add points to hold the shape.
- **Sewing order**: not applicable
- **Finishes and notions**: grading points marked on the CAD
- **Fabrics**: not applicable
- **Fit faults**: too few points leads to curves that lose shape in other sizes.
- **Variants**: #grade-rules, #nest-grading
- **CLO3D**: grading uses points on the pattern outline; add them where the shape changes.
- **Confidence**: concept: verified (teaching sources); lists: trade practice.

## Size charts (EU, UK, US, ASTM) {#size-charts}

- **Aliases**: size tables, body measurement tables, size labels, EN 13402, BS 3666, ASTM D5585; fr: tableaux de tailles
- **Family**: grading
- **Recognize**: tables of body measurements per labelled size, used as the base of grade rules.
- **Pieces**: not applicable
- **Shape**: not a shape. Key facts: EN 13402 labels clothes by body measurement in centimetres (primary
  measure: bust for dresses, blouses and jackets; waist for skirts and trousers; hip as a secondary
  measure), but national numbering (EU 36, 38, 40) is still the usual label. UK older standard BS
  3666 gave sizes 8 to 32; its use was never mandatory. ASTM D5585 (current revision D5585-21) is the US
  table of body measurements for adult female Misses figure type, sizes 00 to 20, in separate inch and
  metric versions; ASTM D5586 gives the table for women aged 55 and older (Misses). These tables give
  body measurements, not garment measurements. Common crude conversion: UK = EU - 28, US = UK - 4 (UK 10 =
  EU 38 = US 6); this varies by brand and is not a standard. Numeric table values are not reproduced
  here (paid standards). The skill's own typical step between two neighbouring sizes is 4 cm of bust, waist
  and hip round (finishes.md); US Misses steps are in inches (about 1 in = 2.5 cm per even size at
  some points, more at larger sizes, per one retailer chart: not a standard). Source: ASTM/ANSI store
  listings, Wikipedia on EN 13402, teaching notes.
- **Drafting**: 1) Choose the chart that matches the target market and figure type. 2) Take the
  measurements for the base size. 3) Build the grade table from the step columns. 4) Keep a separate chart
  for men, children, and plus sizes.
- **Sewing order**: not applicable
- **Finishes and notions**: not applicable
- **Fabrics**: not applicable
- **Fit faults**: labels differ between brands; fit on measurements, not labels.
- **Variants**: #grade-rules, #proportional-grading
- **CLO3D**: avatars follow the size system chosen at import; check the measurement table in CLO.
- **Confidence**: standards identity: verified (store listings); conversion rule: trade practice;

## Lay planning and marker making {#lay-planning-marker-making}

- **Aliases**: marker making, cut planning, lay plan, cut order planning; fr: plan de coupe, tracé de placement
- **Family**: cutting and production
- **Recognize**: a full-scale layout of all pattern pieces for a cut, drawn or printed on paper, or digital.
- **Pieces**: all pieces of every size to be cut, each with its quantity.
- **Shape**: pieces are arranged inside a rectangle of the fabric's usable width (the narrowest roll width,
  less the selvedge) and a length to be minimised; grain lines parallel to the selvedge (within the
  tolerance given by the factory); pieces must not overlap; a small gap equal to the cutting kerf and
  tolerance is kept. Marker efficiency = total piece area x 100 / marker area; 80 to 85 % is considered
  good in the sources found, lower with bias pieces and with stripes, plaids and nap. Lay consumption =
  sum of (marker length x plies) / pieces cut. Source: apparelresources.com articles, teaching notes.
- **Drafting**: 1) Choose the order: sizes and quantities. 2) Combine sizes in one marker where it
  improves the efficiency (mixed-size markers). 3) Place large pieces first, fill gaps with small ones.
  4) Respect grain, nap, and match requirements. 5) Check the piece count. 6) Record length, width,
  efficiency.
- **Sewing order**: not applicable
- **Finishes and notions**: marker paper, plotter, CAD nesting
- **Fabrics**: width variation among rolls means markers are made to the narrowest width; fabric
  with nap or a directional print limits rotation
- **Fit faults**: not applicable
- **Variants**: #single-multi-ply, #nap-one-way, #matching-stripes-plaids, #zero-waste-cutting
- **CLO3D**: CLO's nesting tool can compute a layout and efficiency for a given fabric width.
- **Confidence**: definitions and efficiency range: verified (trade articles); each factory sets its own
  target.

## Single-ply and multi-ply cutting {#single-multi-ply}

- **Aliases**: spreading and cutting, spread, lay, cut by hand or by machine; fr: matelassage, coupe en plusieurs plis
- **Family**: cutting and production
- **Recognize**: one layer cut (single ply) for prints, leather, one-off garments; stacked layers (multi-ply)
  for production.
- **Pieces**: the marker and the fabric layers.
- **Shape**: not a shape. Spreading modes: face-to-face (plies alternate face up, face down, fastest, lowest
  quality control), one-way (each ply laid in the same direction, more waste, needed for nap), and two-way
  (cloth runs back and forth, no cutting between plies, the most efficient, but not for one-direction
  fabrics). Ply height depends on fabric, order size, cutting method and spreader capacity (up to about
  300 plies in some sources; typically much less for hand-cut or delicate fabrics). Plies must be spread with
  low tension: slack makes ridges, tight tension makes pieces shrink after cutting. Source: teaching
  notes (onlineclothingstudy.com, slides).
- **Drafting**: 1) Choose spreading mode from the fabric. 2) Set ply height. 3) Spread each ply to the
  marker length plus a minimum extra. 4) Fix the marker on top. 5) Cut in the order: notches, large
  curves, then straight lines.
- **Sewing order**: not applicable
- **Finishes and notions**: straight knife, band knife, drill, notcher, weights
- **Fabrics**: slippery fabrics need paper between plies or single-ply cutting
- **Fit faults**: piece sizes vary in tall stacks: lower ply height or use a better knife.
- **Variants**: #lay-planning-marker-making, #die-cutting, #laser-cutting
- **CLO3D**: not applicable
- **Confidence**: modes: verified (teaching notes); ply height figures: a source range, not a standard.

## Nap and one-way fabrics {#nap-one-way}

- **Aliases**: directional fabric, pile direction, one-way print, nap layout; fr: tissu a sens, sens du poil
- **Family**: cutting and production
- **Recognize**: velvet, corduroy, velour, brushed cloth, directional prints, shading twills, fur.
- **Pieces**: all pieces share the same direction on the marker.
- **Shape**: marker modes: nap one way (all pieces point in the same direction), nap either way (symmetric
  fabric; rotation 180 degrees allowed), nap up-and-down (one size up, the next size down). Efficiency
  drops with one-way markers because pieces cannot be rotated into gaps. The pattern carries an arrow for
  nap direction. For pile fabrics, the pile direction (up or down) is chosen by the designer: pile
  running up looks darker and richer, running down lighter and silvery (general knowledge). Source:
  teaching notes.
- **Drafting**: 1) Mark nap direction on each piece. 2) Use one-way mode in the marker. 3) Choose
  direction and keep it for all pieces, including facings and collars.
- **Sewing order**: not applicable
- **Finishes and notions**: pile fabrics: use a needle plate and walking foot; press on a needle board
- **Fabrics**: velvet, cord, fur, brushed wool
- **Fit faults**: shade difference between pieces: wrong direction on one piece.
- **Variants**: #lay-planning-marker-making, #matching-stripes-plaids
- **CLO3D**: set the fabric texture direction on each piece.
- **Confidence**: modes: verified (teaching notes); effect of pile direction: from memory.

## Matching stripes and plaids {#matching-stripes-plaids}

- **Aliases**: pattern matching, plaid matching, stripe matching, match points; fr: raccord de rayures et carreaux
- **Family**: cutting and production
- **Recognize**: stripes or checks that continue across seams at centre front, side seams, pockets,
  sleeves and collars.
- **Pieces**: all pieces with match points marked.
- **Shape**: match points are marked on the pattern at the seam positions that must match (notches plus
  match marks at identical bar positions). The same stripe or bar of the fabric is placed at the same
  height on both sides: the second piece is positioned so its match point lies on the same fabric line as
  the first. Horizontal match across CF, side seams and pockets; vertical match for stripes at CF
  and CB. Pattern pieces with curved seams cannot match along the whole seam, only at chosen points.
  Efficiency is lower because pieces must be positioned. Source: trade practice, teaching notes.
- **Drafting**: 1) Choose the repeat and dominant stripe. 2) Mark match points on the pattern. 3) In the
  marker, place pieces so that they hit the same bar. 4) Add extra fabric (more length) to the order for
  matching. 5) Single-ply cut or use pins through plies.
- **Sewing order**: baste seams with the match first; sew with a walking foot to avoid slipping
- **Finishes and notions**: pins, basting thread, walking foot
- **Fabrics**: woven stripes, checks, plaids; yarn-dyed fabrics are more regular than printed ones
- **Fit faults**: stripes look crooked: fabric off-grain; fix at spreading.
- **Variants**: #lay-planning-marker-making, #nap-one-way
- **CLO3D**: texture alignment on each pattern; mirrored textures can be offset for a match.
- **Confidence**: practice: trade practice; extra yardage: not known.

## Bias cutting {#bias-cutting}

- **Aliases**: cut on the bias, true bias, 45 degree cutting; fr: coupe en biais
- **Family**: cutting and production
- **Recognize**: pieces whose grain line is at 45 degrees to the selvedge: bias skirts and dresses, bias
  binding, piping, cowls, circle-skirt portions.
- **Pieces**: any piece with its grain line drawn at 45 degrees.
- **Shape**: the piece is laid at 45 degrees to the selvedge; its grain arrow is marked at 45 degrees
  (finishes.md). The cut is stretchy across and narrows when pulled; side seams drop (grow) while the garment
  hangs. Seams are cut with generous allowance then trimmed after the cloth has hung. Source: skill
  finishes.md and transformations.md (circle skirt: bias parts stretch, let hang 24 hours).
- **Drafting**: 1) Draw the grain line at 45 degrees. 2) Mark which direction is the length. 3)
  Pieces cut in pairs must be mirrored, not rotated. 4) Leave the cut pieces hanging if time permits.
- **Sewing order**: sew bias seams with light tension; hang before hem; use stay tape on necklines
- **Finishes and notions**: narrow rolled hem, fine stitch length, stay tape
- **Fabrics**: silk, satin, crepe, lightweight cotton; heavy fabrics hang unevenly
- **Fit faults**: uneven hem and drooping side seams: hang and level the hem.
- **Variants**: #bias-drape, #adding-flare
- **CLO3D**: set the grain line at 45 degrees; allow for stretch in the simulation.
- **Confidence**: skill references.

## Cutting leather {#cutting-leather}

- **Aliases**: leather layout, hide nesting, leather cutting; fr: coupe du cuir
- **Family**: cutting and production
- **Recognize**: single-ply cutting from an irregular hide with defects.
- **Pieces**: pieces without a fabric grain; the hide has a direction (the backbone) and areas of different
  quality (belly stretches more; the butt is firmer).
- **Shape**: not a shape. Hide edges are irregular; defects (scars, scratches, holes, brands, burn marks) are
  marked before nesting and the pieces are placed to avoid them; important visible pieces take the best area
  and edge pieces the worse area. Yield depends on hide size, quality and the cutter's skill; full-grain
  hide yields less than corrected-grain hide. Computer nesting is said to save about 2 to 4 % over manual
  cutting (a patent summary); vendors claim up to 7 % (marketing figure). Stretch direction: pieces of
  the same garment are cut along the same direction relative to the backbone to avoid shape differences.
  Source: patents and Lectra pages found by search; the stretch rule is from memory.
- **Drafting**: 1) Mark defects. 2) Choose placement areas. 3) Keep the stretch direction the same for
  matching pieces. 4) Cut with a rotary knife, clicker die or laser. 5) Mark with a scribe, not ink,
  on the flesh side.
- **Sewing order**: not applicable
- **Finishes and notions**: no pins in visible leather; use clips and tape; seam allowances are often
  narrower (not known, varies) and glued
- **Fabrics**: cattle, sheep, goat, pig; split leather; synthetic leathers act as wovens or knits
- **Fit faults**: stretching along the belly: re-cut with stable areas.
- **Variants**: #die-cutting, #laser-cutting
- **CLO3D**: set leather as fabric with its own bending and stretch; no grain rule.
- **Confidence**: yield facts: verified (search); stretch rule and allowances: from memory.

## Die cutting {#die-cutting}

- **Aliases**: clicker press cutting, steel rule die, cutting die; fr: decoupe a l'emporte-piece
- **Family**: cutting and production
- **Recognize**: pieces cut by a press with a shaped blade (die) in one stroke, used for repeat items: linings,
  leather goods, shoes, labels, small parts.
- **Pieces**: a die for each piece shape and size; piece notches and holes can be built into the die.
- **Shape**: the die is a closed loop of steel rule blade shaped as the finished outline (no seam allowance
  added at the press: the die outline is the cut line). The die is made from the final pattern, including
  notches and punch holes. A separate die is needed for each size. Source: general trade practice; the
  search found only general nesting information. Details of die construction: unverified.
- **Drafting**: 1) Send the final cut outline, with notches and holes, to the die maker. 2) Fix the
  piece orientation to the grain of the material in the press. 3) Place dies on the material as in a
  marker. 4) Press.
- **Sewing order**: not applicable
- **Finishes and notions**: cutting board, press with adjustable pressure
- **Fabrics**: leather, felt, foam, interlining, labels; stack height limited by the press
- **Fit faults**: dies cannot be corrected: test the pattern before making the die.
- **Variants**: #laser-cutting, #cutting-leather
- **CLO3D**: export the outline from the pattern (DXF) for the die maker.
- **Confidence**: from memory; no source found for details.

## Laser cutting {#laser-cutting}

- **Aliases**: CO2 laser cutting, digital cutting, laser-cut patterns; fr: decoupe laser
- **Family**: cutting and production
- **Recognize**: pieces with sealed, sometimes slightly brown edges, small details and engraved marks.
- **Pieces**: cut outlines in a DXF or similar file; a separate layer for engraved marks.
- **Shape**: the laser follows the outline as a thin line; the beam removes a narrow kerf, so the
  pattern file may be offset by half the kerf; the file's layers decide which lines cut and which
  engrave. Notches and drill points can be burned in or marked as small engraved lines. Kerf width and
  offset: not known (machine-dependent). Source: general knowledge; no source found.
- **Drafting**: 1) Export the outline that must be cut (with seam allowance, since the laser cuts the
  outline it is given). 2) Set the layers: cut, engrave, mark. 3) Test a small piece for edge colour and
  tolerance. 4) Check that pieces do not overlap and that shared edges are not cut twice.
- **Sewing order**: not applicable
- **Finishes and notions**: extraction and ventilation; some synthetics seal at the edge (no fraying),
  natural fibres char
- **Fabrics**: synthetics seal; leather chars; PVC and some coated fabrics release harmful fumes
  (check the material data sheet); natural cloth can scorch
- **Fit faults**: edge burn on natural fibres: reduce power or use another cutting method.
- **Variants**: #die-cutting, #single-multi-ply
- **CLO3D**: export patterns to DXF in the machine's layer convention.
- **Confidence**: from memory; no source found.

## Zero-waste cutting {#zero-waste-cutting}

- **Aliases**: zero waste pattern cutting, jigsaw puzzle method, tessellation, subtraction cutting; fr: coupe zero dechet
- **Family**: cutting and production
- **Recognize**: garments whose pieces fill the fabric length without leftover scraps, often with unusual
  panel shapes, folds and darts moved into seams.
- **Pieces**: variable; the set of pieces forms a rectangle (the fabric width x a chosen length).
- **Shape**: three families are described in the literature: jigsaw puzzle (pieces interlock so that every
  gap is filled, Rissanen and McQuillan), tessellation (shapes repeat and tile) and subtraction cutting
  (cut holes in a flat piece so the cloth can be folded or twisted into the shape, Julian Roberts).
  The pieces must use the entire width and length, and drafting starts from the fabric rectangle, not from
  the block. Off-cuts at normal cutting are estimated at 10 to 15 % of the fabric (a figure attributed to
  Rissanen 2007 in a 2025 conference paper). Specific piece shapes: not given, because each design is
  unique. Source: search results (Turkish study, seamwork, Rissanen UTS chapter); book: Zero Waste Fashion
  Design (Rissanen and McQuillan).
- **Drafting**: 1) Choose the fabric width. 2) Draw the rectangle. 3) Fit the pieces and move
  darts into seams or tucks to fill the gaps. 4) Check that every edge pair has equal length. 5) Test
  in toile and adjust.
- **Sewing order**: depends on the design
- **Finishes and notions**: not applicable
- **Fabrics**: plain wovens with a stable width; prints need the layout in view
- **Fit faults**: fit is constrained: the shapes allow limited adjustment; choose loose styles.
- **Variants**: #lay-planning-marker-making, #kimono-block
- **CLO3D**: draw pieces on a rectangle for a fabric width; check the fit on the avatar.
- **Confidence**: concepts: verified (search results); no design-specific shapes.

## Notches and drill holes {#notches-drill-holes}

- **Aliases**: balance marks, match marks, punch marks, drill points; fr: crans, piquages, repères
- **Family**: cutting and production
- **Recognize**: small cuts at the edge of a piece (notches) and small holes inside (drill holes) which
  show where pieces meet or where something is placed.
- **Pieces**: all pieces.
- **Shape**: notches are 0.5 to 0.6 cm cuts into the allowance (finishes.md), placed on every long seam
  (at least every 20 to 30 cm), at changes of shape (hip, knee) and at gathers or ease. Convention: one notch
  at the front, two at the back, one at the top of the sleeve cap. Darts: notches at each leg, drill hole
  1 cm before the point, inside the dart. Pockets, buttonholes: drill holes at the corners (finishes.md).
  In DXF-AAMA, notches and drill holes are separate layers, and drill holes are points. Source: skill
  finishes.md; search on DXF-AAMA/ASTM layers.
- **Drafting**: 1) Mark the notches on the final cut lines, perpendicular to the edge. 2) Make
  matching notches on mating pieces at the same distance along the seam. 3) Mark drill holes for dart
  points and pockets. 4) Avoid notching inside the seam line.
- **Sewing order**: match notches while pinning
- **Finishes and notions**: notcher, awl, tailor's chalk, thread tacks
- **Fabrics**: fraying fabrics: shorter notches or marks with pen; leather: marks only, never notches in
  thin leather
- **Fit faults**: unclear notch: ambiguous matching; use the single/double/triple code.
- **Variants**: #seam-allowance-conventions, #pattern-annotation-cad-exchange
- **CLO3D**: notches are marks on the pattern; add them as internal points or lines.
- **Confidence**: skill references; CAD layers: secondary sources.

## Seam allowance conventions {#seam-allowance-conventions}

- **Aliases**: seam allowance, hem allowance, sewing allowance; fr: valeur de couture, marge de couture
- **Family**: cutting and production
- **Recognize**: the extra width around the net outline of a piece.
- **Pieces**: all pieces.
- **Shape**: the skill's table (finishes.md): plain seams 1 cm (1.5 cm in industrial production); neckline and
  armhole with facing 0.6 to 1 cm; centre back with zip 1.5 to 2 cm; French seam 1.5 cm; trouser crotch and
  centre back 1.5 to 2.5 cm; straight skirt or dress hem 3 to 4 cm; trouser hem 4 cm; sleeve hem 2 to 3 cm;
  very flared hem 0.5 to 1 cm; edge on the fold 0. In the US home-sewing convention, 5/8 inch (1.5 cm) is
  common (from memory). Allowances are drawn parallel to the net outline; on curves and corners, the
  allowance is mitred or corrected after closing the dart or sewing seam pairs. CLO3D adds its own
  allowance, so the net outline is sent (finishes.md). Source: skill finishes.md.
- **Drafting**: 1) Draw the net outline. 2) Add the allowance per seam type. 3) Square the corners at hem
  and vent. 4) Fold darts and trim the allowance to follow the fold. 5) Label allowance in the
  annotations when it differs from the standard.
- **Sewing order**: not applicable
- **Finishes and notions**: serging, pinking, turning
- **Fabrics**: fraying fabrics need larger allowance; stretch fabrics need less, with overlock
- **Fit faults**: allowances that are not constant produce wrong sizes: sew on the stitch line, not the cut
  edge.
- **Variants**: #notches-drill-holes, #pattern-annotation-cad-exchange
- **CLO3D**: send the net outline; CLO3D adds seam allowance; check the setting.

## Pattern annotation and CAD exchange (DXF-AAMA, DXF-ASTM) {#pattern-annotation-cad-exchange}

- **Aliases**: DXF-AAMA, DXF-ASTM, ASTM D6673, pattern file exchange, pattern markings; fr: echange de fichiers patron
- **Family**: cutting and production
- **Recognize**: .dxf pattern files with several layers (cut line, notches, grain line, drill holes, text)
  exchanged between CAD systems (Gerber, Lectra, Optitex, Clo, others).
- **Pieces**: every piece with name, size, number to cut, grain line, notches, drill holes and, if present,
  grade rules.
- **Shape**: not a shape. Pattern annotation (finishes.md): name, size, quantity, "on fold" when relevant,
  fabric direction when nap exists, grain line parallel to the selvedge (45 degrees for bias). ASTM D6673
  is a DXF-based practice (AutoCAD version 13 DXF); it specifies that the pattern file complies with DXF. A
  drill hole is a point that is part of a piece but not part of any line; drill holes may mark pocket or
  buttonhole placement. The grade rules are exchanged in a separate ASCII rule table file in the ASTM form
  (the AAMA files embed them as movement values). Layers in AAMA files, reported in secondary sources:
  1 cut outline, 2 turn or grade points, 4 notch, 8 draw/ink, 11 internal cut, 13 drill, 14 sew line,
  19 text, 26 reference. These layer numbers come from forum posts and a Scribd document and were
  not checked against the AAMA publication. D6673 revision 2010 was withdrawn in 2019 (a catalogue listing).
  Source: ASTM store/ANSI listings, Minerva blog, rhino forum, Scribd (as noted).
- **Drafting**: 1) Name every piece and set quantity. 2) Put cut lines, notches, drill holes and grain
  lines on the layers expected by the receiver. 3) Send a sample file to the receiver and ask for its
  own sample. 4) Test import of a simple piece before a full set.
- **Sewing order**: not applicable
- **Finishes and notions**: rule table file, a pattern list (name, size, cut count)
- **Fabrics**: not applicable
- **Fit faults**: scale error from unit mismatch: check a 10 cm test square (finishes.md).
- **Variants**: #notches-drill-holes, #seam-allowance-conventions
- **CLO3D**: import and export DXF (AAMA/ASTM) from the file menu; test with a small piece; check units and
  that seam allowances are not double-counted.
- **Confidence**: ASTM D6673 identity and drill-hole definition: verified (search); layer numbers:
  secondary, unverified; ask the receiving system for a sample file.
