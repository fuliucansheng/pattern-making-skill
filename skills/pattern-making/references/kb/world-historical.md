# World and historical garments

Scope: East Asia, South Asia, Middle East and North Africa, West Africa, European folk dress, the Americas, the Pacific, and historical European dress. Many of these garments are cut from rectangles, gores and gussets, so most **Shape** fields give a cutting layout rather than a body-fitted outline.

Reading the confidence tags: "drawn pattern" means the layout is documented in a published pattern or museum record; "documented" means a museum catalogue or published source describes it in words; "reconstructed practice" means current reenactment or tailoring practice; "unverified" means taken from general knowledge without a drawn source. Garment names belong to living or historical cultures; regional variants differ and the layouts below describe the common form only.

## Kimono (women's, adult) {#kimono}

- **Aliases**: wafuku (garment family), kitsuke-ready kimono; fr: kimono japonais
- **Family**: Japanese straight-cut garments (tan-based)
- **Recognize**: T-shape laid flat, straight seams only, right panel wraps over left (left over right only for the dead), wide overlapping front, separate collar band (eri) running from nape down both fronts, rectangular sleeves with a rounded outer-bottom corner (sode), a seam at center back, a sleeve opening (miyatsuguchi) under the armhole and a slit (furi) below it, obi worn over the waist.
- **Pieces**: body panel (migoro) (2), okumi / front overlap strip (2), collar (eri) (1), sleeve (sode) (2), collar overlay (kakeeri) (1, optional, protects the collar)
- **Shape**: One standard roll (tan) is 36-38 cm wide and about 11.4-12 m long; men's rolls are 38-40 cm (documented: Bunka Fashion Research Institute yukata pages; Mueller & Sohn). All pieces are rectangles cut across or along the roll at full width, so there is almost no waste.
  Body panels (migoro): two full-width rectangles, each as long as front plus back (about twice the body length from shoulder fold to hem). Each is folded at the shoulder line; the two panels are sewn together along the center back (a long vertical seam). The shoulder is a fold, not a seam. A neck opening is cut at the fold, on each side of the center back seam, for the eri to attach to.
  Okumi: two strips cut from a full-width piece split lengthwise (half the cloth width each, documented). Each okumi sews to the front edge of its body panel from the hem to just below the shoulder fold, adding the front width.
  Eri: one long strip, folded lengthwise, sewn to the neck edge of the body and down the okumi front edges.
  Sleeve (sode): rectangle cut across the cloth width, folded in half to make a sleeve; the sleeve opening edge is left open for the hand (tamoto), the outer bottom corner is rounded, the sleeve is sewn to the body along the shoulder line leaving a gap for the armhole (miyatsuguchi). The sewn length and the open length sum to the sleeve width.
  Numeric proportions of the finished garment (back-center-to-cuff, sleeve length/width, collar width) were not obtained as a verified table; the Okayama CAD paper cited below lists values in a garbled scan (collar width 7.5, front panel 15, sleeve width 33, sleeve depth 48) and must be checked against the original. The Mueller & Sohn notes give an armhole front length of 112 cm and a collar neck depth of 14-16 cm with a finished collar width of 5-9 cm. Unverified as a set.
- **Drafting**: 1. Body length (mi-take) is measured to suit the wearer and the obi fold (traditionally about the wearer's height for the ohashori, typical). 2. Yuki (back-center to wrist with arm extended) sets half the sleeve-to-sleeve span: yuki = back panel half-width + sleeve width. 3. Cut the roll at full width with no shaped edges; notch the shoulder fold and sleeve joins. 4. Seam allowance on the long seams is small and constant (typical about 1.5-2 cm in the narrow strips), and the back-center and sleeve joins are hand-sewn in Japanese tailoring.
- **Sewing order**: 1. sew the center-back seam of the body panels. 2. sew the sleeve outer edges and attach them to the body leaving the armhole opening. 3. sew okumi to the body fronts. 4. sew eri to the neck and front edges. 5. sew the side seams below the arm opening. 6. hem and sew the collar overlay.
- **Finishes and notions**: hand-sewn running stitch; hems turned in; collar stiffened with a non-woven or wool interfacing; no zip or buttons. Tying cords (datejime) and obi supply closure.
- **Fabrics**: silk, silk crepe (chirimen), wool, cotton; width is fixed by the loom; because the bolt width is the pattern width, a wider fabric changes only the number of rectangles, not the construction. Kimono silk is often kept uncut for resale and re-sewing (the kimono is traditionally unpicked and reused).
- **Fit faults**: body too short for the obi -> lengthen panels and okumi; yuki too long -> shorten the sleeve joining length (the sleeve width can be reduced), never narrow the back panel.
- **Variants**: #yukata (unlined cotton), #haori (open jacket), #jinbei (two-piece summer). Furisode (long-sleeved kimono) lengthens the sleeve rectangle; the body layout is the same.
- **CLO3D**: build each rectangle as a pattern piece in the order above; sew okumi to body, then the eri along the neck and fronts; attach sleeves with the armhole opening free; use a fabric with a stiffer bend than normal silk for the obi area if you model an obi. Mesh 10-15 mm. The user must place the wrap (right over left) and the obi by hand.
- **Confidence**: documented (Bunka Fashion Research Institute; Mueller & Sohn); piece names and count verified; finished dimensions unverified.

## Yukata {#yukata}

- **Aliases**: summer kimono, cotton kimono; fr: yukata
- **Family**: Japanese straight-cut garments
- **Recognize**: unlined cotton kimono construction, often in indigo or bold prints, worn belted with a soft obi (hanhaba or heko), no padded collar; sleeves rectangular with the same rounded corner.
- **Pieces**: sleeve (sode) (2), body panel (migoro) (2), collar (eri) (1), front overlap strip (okumi) (2), collar overlay (kakeeri) (1)
- **Shape**: Identical in layout to #kimono. Documented by the Bunka Fashion Research Institute: one roll of cloth 36 cm wide and 1140-1200 cm long makes one yukata, with king-size rolls of 38-40 cm common for men's. The okumi are cut at half the cloth width. Cutting method (documented): lay both ends of the roll side by side, fold the pattern pieces two-ply, estimate body, sleeve and okumi lengths, and if the arrangement does not fit, plan the sleeves first. Confirm the total length before cutting. The page does not state the individual dimensions. Unverified numerically.
- **Drafting**: As #kimono; yukata are unlined so allow no extra allowance for lining. The fixed roll length limits the total of body lengths, sleeve lengths and okumi lengths.
- **Sewing order**: 1. cut the roll. 2. mark with chalk or thread tracing. 3. sew the center back. 4. attach sleeves. 5. attach okumi. 6. attach eri. 7. hem. 8. finish (documented as "cutting, marking, sewing, finishing" in the Bunka lesson; step detail not obtained).
- **Finishes and notions**: running stitch, turned-in hems, no interfacing in cheap versions or a lightweight collar interlining.
- **Fabrics**: cotton, cotton-linen, light wool; nap and one-way prints need the same direction for all pieces because the roll is cut lengthwise.
- **Fit faults**: as #kimono.
- **Variants**: #kimono, #jinbei, hanten (padded jacket).
- **CLO3D**: as #kimono with cotton preset; thinner bend.
- **Confidence**: documented (piece list and cloth dimensions); layout unverified.

## Haori {#haori}

- **Aliases**: kimono jacket; fr: haori
- **Family**: Japanese straight-cut garments
- **Recognize**: hip-to-thigh-length open jacket, collar folded back along the front (the eri is turned out), sleeves like the kimono, side gussets (machi), two ties (haori-himo) at the front edge, no overlap.
- **Pieces**: body panel (2), sleeve (2), collar (eri) (2, folded back), side gusset / machi (2), tie loops (himo) (2)
- **Shape**: Unverified layout, from general knowledge: a shorter version of the #kimono body without okumi, because the haori hangs open. The extra width at the hem comes from the machi (vertical gussets) inserted at the side seams from the hem to the underarm; they add width without changing the sleeve line. The collar is cut as a long strip and folded back at the neck so that the front shows the lining. Dimensions not known.
- **Drafting**: 1. Body length to suit (typical hip to mid-thigh). 2. Sleeve width and length as #kimono. 3. Machi width typical 5-7 cm finished (unverified). 4. Collar strip as long as the front edge plus neck, folded in half.
- **Sewing order**: 1. back seam. 2. sleeves. 3. machi to side seams. 4. collar folded and sewn on. 5. himo attached. 6. hem.
- **Finishes and notions**: lining usually of contrasting silk; silk cords.
- **Fabrics**: silk, wool, cotton; lined versions need the lining cut as one more set of pieces.
- **Fit faults**: hangs too narrow at the hem -> widen the machi, not the body.
- **Variants**: #kimono (full length wrap), happi (festival coat).
- **CLO3D**: build as open jacket; do not sew fronts together; machi as separate panels sewn to the side edges.
- **Confidence**: from memory; shape unverified.

## Hakama {#hakama}

- **Aliases**: pleated divided skirt/trousers; umanori (divided), andon (undivided); fr: hakama
- **Family**: Japanese trousers/skirts
- **Recognize**: wide pleated garment worn over a kimono from waist to ankle, front pleats and back pleats, back stiffener (koshiita), ties crossing the waist, side openings.
- **Pieces**: front panel (1), back panel (1), front ties (2), back ties (2), back stiffener (koshiita) (1)
- **Shape**: Unverified. From general knowledge: front and back panels are trapezoids wider at the hem, sewn along both sides leaving the upper sides open (the openings are called yatsuguchi on the kimono-wearing side, so the underlying kimono can be adjusted). Front pleats: 5 (documented in general sources as "five pleats at the front and two at the back" for the standard form). Umanori has a central seam dividing it into two legs; andon is a tube with no crotch seam. Dimensions not known.
- **Drafting**: 1. Waist = wearer's waist plus overlap. 2. Hem width approximately 2-3 times the waist (typical, unverified). 3. Pleat depth set to bring the waist width to the band. 4. Length from waist to ankle.
- **Sewing order**: 1. hem. 2. stitch side seams below the opening. 3. pleat the waist. 4. sew the waist ties/bands. 5. attach koshiita to back.
- **Finishes and notions**: stiff back board; cotton ties.
- **Fabrics**: stiff cotton, silk twill, striped hakama cloth (kenroku).
- **Fit faults**: pleats splay -> press permanently; hem rises in front -> lengthen front.
- **Variants**: #kimono, #jinbei (shorts).
- **CLO3D**: build as two flat panels; pleat as fold lines; then sew the openings.
- **Confidence**: from memory; shape unverified.

## Jeogori (hanbok jacket) {#jeogori}

- **Aliases**: hanbok jacket, Korean upper garment; fr: jeogori
- **Family**: Korean traditional garments
- **Recognize**: short jacket ending at or above the waist (women's) or hip (men's), curved sleeve seams (baerae), a crossing collar band (git) with a white removable collar (dongjeong), two ties (goreum) at the chest, contrasting cuffs (kkeutdong) on women's jackets, a diagonal front.
- **Pieces**: body panel / gil (front and back sections, 2 front + 1 back), sleeve (2), collar band / git (2), removable collar / dongjeong (1), ties / goreum (2), gusset / mu (2, under sleeve)
- **Shape**: Documented in words only: gil are the body panels, git is the collar band that trims the neck and front, dongjeong sits over the end of the git, goreum tie the front and have moved to the front of the garment since the Joseon dynasty (Wikipedia, museum catalogues). Sleeves have curved seams (baerae) rather than straight ones. No dimensioned drawn pattern was obtained. Unverified.
- **Drafting**: Not known from a drawn source. Typical hanbok drafting uses height-derived proportions (back-center length, sleeve length from the shoulder).
- **Sewing order**: unverified.
- **Finishes and notions**: hand-sewn; goreum are narrow tubes; dongjeong replaced after washing.
- **Fabrics**: silk, ramie, hemp, cotton; sheer fabrics lined or double.
- **Fit faults**: unverified.
- **Variants**: #chima-hanbok (skirt), baji (men's trousers, not covered).
- **CLO3D**: build body panels first, then add git and sleeves with a curved seam; ties as long thin rectangles. The user places the cross and tie by hand.
- **Confidence**: documented in words only; shape unverified.

## Chima (hanbok skirt) {#chima-hanbok}

- **Aliases**: Korean wrap skirt, hanbok skirt; fr: chima
- **Family**: Korean traditional garments
- **Recognize**: high-waisted full skirt starting just under the bust, wrapped around the body, pleated or gathered onto a narrow waistband (mal) with long ties, worn over underskirts that give volume.
- **Pieces**: skirt panel (3.5-4), waistband (mal) (1), ties (2)
- **Shape**: Documented from museum records: a Korean skirt in the UBC Museum of Anthropology is made of three and a half loom-width panels, each about 72 cm wide (so the skirt is 3.5 panels wide), pleated onto a white waistband with long ties; a child's skirt uses four panels of silk sewn with running stitch. The skirt wraps; the open edge is finished with stitching because four panels would be too bulky. Other museum pieces use four panels. Length and waistband dimensions are not given. A modern machine-made example is gathered to a waistband that connects to straps and lined with white cotton for volume.
- **Drafting**: 1. Panels are loom width (typical 72 cm in the cited example). 2. Total panel count 3.5-4. 3. Waist circumference = pleat-compressed width; pleat depth = panel width total minus waist. 4. Length from underbust to floor or ankle.
- **Sewing order**: 1. join panels with selvedges. 2. hem. 3. pleat or gather to waist. 4. attach to waistband with ties.
- **Finishes and notions**: invisible hem stitching, white cotton lining in modern forms.
- **Fabrics**: silk, ramie; lining for volume.
- **Fit faults**: skirt sags at the waist -> reduce the gather or add a support strap.
- **Variants**: #jeogori (jacket), #lehenga (South Asian gored skirt).
- **CLO3D**: build panels as rectangles sewn at selvedge, pleat at the waistband; fabric weight light; add a petticoat layer.
- **Confidence**: documented for panel count and width; other dimensions unverified.

## Qipao / cheongsam {#qipao}

- **Aliases**: cheongsam, mandarin gown, Chinese fitted dress; fr: qipao
- **Family**: Chinese garments
- **Recognize**: long fitted sheath dress with a high stand (mandarin) collar, asymmetrical diagonal closure (da jin) from the neck to the right underarm, then down the right side with frogs (pankou) or buttons, side slits at the hem, short or no sleeves in the Republic-era form.
- **Pieces**: front (1, with the overlapping right front joined), back (1 or 2), sleeve (2, if any), collar (1), facing/binding (continuous)
- **Shape**: Unverified. From general knowledge: the Republic-era form (1920s onward) shapes the torso with bust darts and waist darts or side-shaping seams, and the right front overlaps the left with a curved diagonal (da jin) line from the collar down to the underarm. The side slits begin at the hem and reach mid-thigh to calf. No drawn source obtained.
- **Drafting**: Typical modern drafting follows a close-fitting dress block: bust and waist darts, a stand collar 3-4 cm high (typical), shoulder slope close to the neck, and the overlap placed with the da jin curve. Numbers not verified.
- **Sewing order**: 1. darts and side seams. 2. shoulders. 3. collar. 4. overlap closure with frogs. 5. slits and hem. 6. sleeves/binding.
- **Finishes and notions**: bias-cut binding in matching or contrasting silk; hand-made knot buttons (pankou) and loops; hook-and-eye or invisible zip at the side seam in modern forms.
- **Fabrics**: silk brocade, crepe, cotton; stretch fabrics need less ease and alter the slit and dart pattern.
- **Fit faults**: collar gaps -> lengthen the neck curve; diagonal line twists -> cut the overlap on the bias grain.
- **Variants**: #changshan (man's gown), #ao-dai (Vietnamese cousin with side slits to the waist).
- **CLO3D**: build as a fitted dress with the right front as an extra panel sewn at the side and neck; a bias binding by hand or a fabric strip.
- **Confidence**: from memory; shape unverified.

## Hanfu ruqun (basics) {#hanfu-ruqun}

- **Aliases**: ru-qun, Han Chinese dress, upper garment and skirt; fr: hanfu
- **Family**: Chinese garments
- **Recognize**: cross-collar (jiaoling youren) upper garment (ru) with right side overlapping left, long straight wide sleeves, and a separate long skirt (qun) tied high on the chest or at the waist.
- **Pieces**: upper garment body (1 piece with center back seam), sleeve (2), collar band (1), tie (2), skirt panels (4-8), waistband (1)
- **Shape**: Unverified. Traditional Han garments are cut from straight rectangles: the body panel is made from lengths seamed at the center back, with the sleeve cut in one with the body on the straight grain (the sleeve line is perpendicular to the center line). The collar is a straight band that crosses on the front. The skirt is a set of panels pleated or gathered into a waistband. Regional and period variants differ widely (Ming, Song, Tang); a specific style should be built from its own reference.
- **Drafting**: Not known.
- **Sewing order**: unverified.
- **Finishes and notions**: silk ties, collar binding.
- **Fabrics**: silk, gauze, brocade; ramie for summer.
- **Fit faults**: unverified.
- **Variants**: #jeogori, #kimono (shared rectangular sleeve logic but different lengths and proportions).
- **CLO3D**: build body and sleeve as straight rectangles; sew sleeves in-line with the body; skirt as separate panels pleated at the waist.
- **Confidence**: from memory; shape unverified.

## Saree blouse (choli) {#saree-blouse}

- **Aliases**: sari blouse, choli, ravike (Kannada), jacket blouse; fr: corsage de sari, choli
- **Family**: South Asian garments (fitted short bodice worn under the saree)
- **Recognize**: short fitted blouse ending at or just below the underbust or waist, usually cut with princess seams or bust darts front and back, set-in short or elbow sleeves, a closure of hooks or a zip at centre front or centre back, often a deep back neckline, cups sometimes padded.
- **Pieces**: front panel (2, each with princess seam or bust and waist darts), back panel (2, split at centre back for the opening), sleeve (2), neck and armhole facings (2-3), optional side panel (2, for the princess cut), cup lining or padding (as needed)
- **Shape**: The blouse is a fitted bodice block cut short, not a rectangular garment. Front: waist-length bodice with a bust dart, or a princess seam running from the armhole or shoulder through the bust apex to the hem; the front neck is deeper than the back. Back: two panels, each shaped with a waist dart or a back princess seam; the centre back is straight (zip or hooks) or carries an overlap; the back neck is shallower than the front but is often scooped low as a design choice. Sleeve: a standard set-in sleeve block shortened. Source: general fitted-bodice practice and Indian vocational dress-making manuals (CTS Dress Making trade practical, NIMI); no dimensioned drawing was read, so the numbers are unverified.
- **Drafting**: 1. Take bust, underbust or waist, armhole, shoulder, front length, back length, front and back neck depth, sleeve length. 2. Draft a close-fitting bodice from a basic block (see #close-fitting-bodice-block) shortened at the waist or just below the underbust. 3. Place the princess seam from armhole or shoulder through the bust apex to the hem, or use a bust dart and a waist dart. 4. Seam allowances in one free online tutorial: about 6 mm at neckline and centre front, 12 mm at shoulder, armhole and hem, and about 38 mm at the side seams (a wide side allowance is conventional in Indian tailoring so the blouse can be let out). 5. Add allowance at the centre back or centre front for the closure.
- **Sewing order**: 1. interline or pad the cups. 2. sew darts or princess seams. 3. shoulder seams. 4. face or bind the neckline. 5. set the sleeves. 6. side seams. 7. closure (hooks and eyes, zip, or tie-backs). 8. hem.
- **Finishes and notions**: hooks and eyes, zip, piping on the neckline, cup padding, tie-back cords.
- **Fabrics**: silk, cotton, brocade, georgette with lining; stretch fabric needs less ease.
- **Fit faults**: neckline gapes -> add a shaped neck dart or reduce the front neck width; armhole cuts into the arm -> lower the underarm and widen the sleeve; blouse rides up under the pleated saree -> lengthen the bodice at the hem.
- **Variants**: #sari-petticoat (the saree base), #lehenga (also worn with a choli), #anarkali.
- **CLO3D**: build front, side-front and back panels as a fitted bodice with princess seams; sew shoulders and sides; silk or cotton preset; the saree drape itself is an unsewn rectangle (about 5-9 m long) that the user must place by hand.
- **Confidence**: trade practice / vocational manual; numbers unverified.

## Sari petticoat {#sari-petticoat}

- **Aliases**: saree petticoat, inskirt, parkar or pavadai (south India); fr: jupon de sari
- **Family**: South Asian undergarments and skirts
- **Recognize**: floor-length A-line slip worn under the saree, plain, with a drawstring (nada) waist and no darts.
- **Pieces**: skirt gore (kali) (4 or 6), waist casing (1), drawstring (1)
- **Shape**: A skirt cut in gores that taper to the waist and flare to the hem so it does not cling when the saree pleats are tucked into the waist. Each gore is a long narrow trapezoid (narrow at the waist, wider at the hem) cut with the straight grain along its centre line; the gores are joined along the long edges and the waist is turned into a casing. One seam is left partly open as a placket if the waist is not wide enough to pass over the hips. Source: general Indian tailoring practice; no drawn pattern read. Unverified numerically.
- **Drafting**: 1. Waist = waist measurement plus enough circumference to pass over the hips. 2. Length = waist to floor (or to the saree hem). 3. Divide waist and hem circumference by the gore count and add seam allowances. 4. Hem sweep: not known; the flare must be enough for walking.
- **Sewing order**: 1. join the gores along long edges. 2. leave one seam partly open if needed. 3. turn and stitch the waist casing. 4. thread the drawstring. 5. hem.
- **Finishes and notions**: drawstring; usually unlined.
- **Fabrics**: cotton poplin or cotton-polyester; the weight must not be so stiff that the saree pleats stand out.
- **Fit faults**: petticoat shows at the hem -> shorten; saree pleats slip -> tighten the casing.
- **Variants**: #gored-skirt, #lehenga (a decorative gored skirt).
- **CLO3D**: gores as trapezoids sewn at the long edges; waist as a casing; light cotton preset.
- **Confidence**: from memory / trade practice; numbers unverified.

## Salwar {#salwar}

- **Aliases**: shalwar, Punjabi salwar, patiala salwar (very full), Afghani; fr: salwar
- **Family**: South Asian trousers
- **Recognize**: wide trousers gathered or pleated at the waist with a drawstring, narrowing to an ankle band or hem; a crotch gusset allowing a wide stride; legs very full (patiala) or straighter.
- **Pieces**: leg panel (2), crotch gusset (1 diamond or 2 triangles), waist casing (1), drawstring (1), ankle band (2, optional)
- **Shape**: Two leg panels, each with a rounded front crotch and a deeper back crotch curve; a vocational drafting sheet gives the crotch depth as one third of the hip circumference plus 5 cm (low-reliability mirrored PDF, to be verified before use) and cuts the trouser on the fold. Traditional salwar add a square or diamond gusset in the crotch (general descriptions of the garment, not dimensioned). The patiala salwar uses a much wider panel with pleats at the waist, and some are drafted as a semi-circular panel for the fullness. No museum drawn pattern was read. Unverified numerically.
- **Drafting**: 1. Measure waist, hip, crotch depth (rise), outer length, ankle opening. 2. Panel width at the hip = hip/2 plus generous ease (the amount depends on the style; not known). 3. Crotch depth = hip/3 + 5 cm (reported value). 4. Narrow to the ankle band or hem. 5. Cut the gusset as a square whose side matches the crotch gap.
- **Sewing order**: 1. inseams. 2. set the crotch gusset. 3. centre seams. 4. waist casing. 5. ankle band or hem. 6. thread the drawstring.
- **Finishes and notions**: drawstring or elastic casing, lace or piping at the hem, optional side pockets.
- **Fabrics**: cotton, silk, crepe, georgette; a drapey fabric makes patiala pleats sit softly.
- **Fit faults**: crotch pulls -> lengthen the rise at the back crotch curve; baggy knees -> narrow the leg below the knee.
- **Variants**: #churidar (tight, ruched at the ankle), #kameez (the tunic worn with it), #hakama (Japanese pleated trouser).
- **CLO3D**: two leg panels sewn at the inseam, crotch seam and gusset diamond; pleat the waist with fold lines; light cotton or silk preset; mesh 10 mm.
- **Confidence**: documented in words (vocational sheets); numbers unverified.

## Kameez (salwar suit tunic) {#kameez}

- **Aliases**: Punjabi suit top, long tunic; fr: kameez
- **Family**: South Asian tunics
- **Recognize**: knee-length tunic with side slits (chaak) at the hem, straight or lightly shaped side seams, round or V neckline, straight or set-in sleeves, usually a gusset under the arm.
- **Pieces**: front (1), back (1), sleeve (2), underarm gusset (2, square), neck facing (1), optional placket (1)
- **Shape**: In the traditional form the front and back are near-rectangles seamed at the shoulders and sides, with a square gusset set into the underarm to widen the arm opening and ease movement. The only dimensioned example found is a child's kalidar tunic in a school textbook (see #kurta): gusset 8 x 8 cm; that must not be applied to adult sizes. Source: general vocational practice. Unverified for adult sizes.
- **Drafting**: 1. Take chest, waist, hip, shoulder, length, sleeve length, armhole. 2. Half-width of front or back = (chest + ease)/4 plus seam allowance (typical convention in Indian tailoring; ease value varies). 3. Armhole depth = chest/4 plus 2-3 cm (typical, unverified). 4. Slits to about hip level. 5. Front neck depth and back neck depth by style.
- **Sewing order**: 1. shoulders. 2. neck facing. 3. sleeves flat. 4. side seams with gussets, slits left open. 5. hem and slit stitching.
- **Finishes and notions**: bound neckline, piping, front placket if buttoned; slit corners reinforced.
- **Fabrics**: cotton, silk, georgette, chiffon (line or underline sheer fabrics).
- **Fit faults**: drag lines from the armhole -> widen the gusset; slits gape -> narrow the slit or add a stay.
- **Variants**: #kurta (also worn alone, men's), #anarkali, #salwar and #churidar (worn with it).
- **CLO3D**: front and back as rectangles with shaped armhole; gusset as a square set diagonally; keep slits open; cotton preset.
- **Confidence**: trade practice; adult numbers unverified.

- **Aliases**: churidar pyjama, churidar leggings; fr: churidar

- **Shape**: A fitted trouser leg with thigh ease and a slender calf and ankle. The leg is cut much longer than the wearer's leg so that it bunches at the ankle. - **Fabrics**: cotton, cotton-lycra, silk; a bias cut gives stretch; stretch fabrics reduce the need for the bias.

## Kurta {#kurta}

- **Aliases**: Indian long shirt, kurta pyjama (with pyjama); fr: kurta

- **Shape**: The straight kurta is a T of rectangles: front and back cut in one with the shoulder, sleeves as rectangles sewn to the straight armhole, a square gusset set into the underarm, side slits starting at the hem. - **Variants**: #kameez (women's), #sherwani (formal coat), #nehru-jacket.
- **CLO3D**: rectangles with shaped armhole; gusset set diagonally; placket as an overlap.
- **Confidence**: documented for the child-size kali and gusset; adult drafting unverified.

- **Aliases**: ghagra, lehnga, ghaghra choli (the set), pavada (southern); fr: lehenga

## Abaya {#abaya}

- **Aliases**: aba, open abaya (kimono abaya), closed abaya, butterfly abaya; fr: abaya
- **Family**: Arabian Peninsula and Gulf robes
- **Recognize**: floor-length loose robe, usually black, T-shaped, long sleeves, open at the front (worn over clothes) or closed with a front zip or snaps; sleeves cut in one with the body or set in, often with a cuff; hem wide enough to walk.
- **Pieces**: front (2 if open, or 1 cut on the fold if closed), back (1, on the fold or with a centre-back seam), sleeve (2, or cut in one with the body), neck or front facing (1), cuff (2, optional)
- **Shape**: In modern commercial forms the body is a long rectangle folded over the shoulder line, with the sleeve cut in one with the body as a slanted T (the underarm angle sets the sleeve fullness), or with a separate straight sleeve sewn to a low armhole. Source: general modern abaya cutting practice; no museum drawn pattern or published dimensions were read. Unverified.

Sleeve slope: draw the sleeve as a line from the neck point at about 10-25 degrees below horizontal for a T-sleeve (typical range; unverified). 4. Flare 5-15 cm at the hem per side (typical, unverified). 5. Front neckline either a slit or round neck 8-9 cm drop (typical).

- **Variants**: #kaftan, #thobe-kandura, #kimono (compare the T-shape).
- **CLO3D**: fold-based T-shape; sleeve in one with the body; large bending length; if the abaya is open, do not sew the front edges.

## Boubou {#boubou}

- **Aliases**: grand boubou, mbubb (Wolof), agbada (Yoruba, three-piece variant), babariga (Hausa); fr: boubou
- **Family**: West African robes
- **Recognize**: very wide, long robe with a large neck opening and wide open sides, embroidered at the neck and chest, worn by men and women with different neck shapes (round for women, V for men) over trousers or a wrapper.
- **Pieces**: body (1, a single rectangle folded in half), neck facing (1), optional embroidered panel (1), optional pocket (1)
- **Shape**: Documented by the Fashion Institute of Technology fashion history entry: the boubou is made of one large rectangle of fabric with an opening in the centre for the neck; the fabric is usually about 150 cm (59 in) wide and of varying length; the grand boubou typically uses a piece about 300 cm (117 in) long and reaches the ankles. Strips: the body width divided by the strip width gives the strip count (for example, 150 cm / 10.8 cm = about 14 strips; arithmetic only).

- **Fabrics**: bazin riche (damask cotton), lace, cotton, strip-woven cloth.
- **Fit faults**: neck falls off one shoulder -> narrow the neck opening; sleeves feel short -> raise the side-seam stop.
- **Variants**: #kaftan, #dashiki, #poncho.
- **CLO3D**: single rectangle with a neck hole, sides sewn from the hem to the underarm; a stiffer bending preset for bazin.
- **Confidence**: documented (FIT; museum records); finished dimensions vary by wearer.

## Kilt (Highland kilt, pleated to the sett or to the stripe) {#kilt}

- **Aliases**: Highland kilt, great kilt (feileadh mor, the earlier belted plaid), small kilt (feileadh beag); fr: kilt
- **Family**: Scottish wrapped and pleated skirts (men's and women's)
- **Recognize**: knee-length wraparound skirt in tartan with flat plain aprons at the front and deep knife pleats from the side-fronts around the back; a fringed outer apron edge; two or three buckled straps at the left hip; a deep waistband; the left apron wraps under the right, and the right apron sits on top.
- **Pieces**: tartan length (1, full length; pleats are made in it, the aprons are plain), outer apron (1), inner apron (1), waistband stiffener / canvas (1), fringe strip (optional), straps and buckles (2-3)
- **Shape**: A single long piece of tartan whose length runs around the body, the width of cloth becoming the finished kilt length; at each end an apron, plain or with the pattern matched at the centre. The middle is knife-pleated so that the tartan repeat (sett) shows on the pleats. Documented in a Scottish dance kilt-making guide (scottishdance.net): each pleat uses one full sett width plus about 1 inch of exposed pleat, so an 8-inch sett consumes 9 inches per pleat; each apron is 21 inches for a 42-inch hip; the worked example for a 42-inch hip totals 277 inches (3.86 yd, rounded to 4 yd) after adding 20 percent for extra pleating and centring the apron pattern; a 6-inch sett needs about 3.15 yd; a 56-inch hip with an 8-inch sett needs just over 5 yd. The same guide splits the hip-waist difference: half is taken in with darts at the apron sides, and half is spread across the pleats; the pleat tops are trimmed inside, from the waist to 1 inch above the hip line, in a strip 1.5-2 inches deep; the waistband is a strip 5 inches wide; the fringe strip 3 inches wide; buckles go about 2 inches from the fringed side. Pleat to the sett means each pleat shows the pattern so the sett repeats around the kilt; pleat to the stripe centres a prominent stripe on every pleat (regimental form). Maker pages (kilts-n-stuff, St Kilda store) say a 4-yard casual kilt fits up to a waist of about 34 in, a 5-yard to about 44 in, a 6-yard to about 50 in (box pleats use less cloth), with the cut-offs approximate. Source: scottishdance.net "Making a Kilt"; maker pages. Selvedge-as-hem and the choice of grain on the pleat are trade practice (verify with your cloth).
- **Drafting**: 1. Measure the waist, the hip, and the length from waist (at the natural waist) to mid-knee; add about 2 inches if a wide belt is worn (reported). 2. Sett size from the cloth (count the repeat on the warp). 3. Pleat consumption = sett + about 1 inch. 4. Number of pleats and their depth so that the pleated part fits the waist after the aprons and their tapering. 5. Total length = 2 aprons + pleats + 20 percent (reported). 6. Taper the pleats inward from the hip to the waist. 7. Sew the fell (the stitched part of each pleat) from the waist to hip level, then release.
- **Sewing order**: 1. mark the apron and pleat positions with a template; 2. fold the pleats and baste with two rows of running stitches; 3. taper at the waist; 4. sew the pleats flat to the fell (blind stitch, about 10 stitches per inch is reported); 5. canvas and waistband; 6. fringe on the outer apron; 7. straps and buckles; 8. press.
- **Finishes and notions**: canvas stiffener, a hand-felled hem or the selvedge, fringe, straps with buckles, a kilt pin on the outer apron.
- **Fabrics**: kilt-weight tartan: about 13-16 oz for a men's kilt, about 10 oz for dancers (reported); heavier cloth needs wider aprons because the pleat stack thickens.
- **Fit faults**: kilt rides up -> the fell is too short, or hip too tight; apron gaps -> widen the apron and balance the darts; pleats splay -> press permanently, and check that the taper is sewn straight.
- **Variants**: #gored-skirt (compare), #hakama (also pleated, Japanese), #pencil-skirt. The great kilt (belted plaid) is a very long unsewn length of tartan pleated by hand and held with a belt; not dimensioned here.
- **CLO3D**: build the kilt as a long rectangle with a repeating pleat structure; set pleats as fold lines (use a pleating tool or many internal lines) and sew the fell by shirring/seams; wool tartan preset with heavy weight; mesh 10 mm; apron wrapped and lapped manually.
- **Confidence**: documented (scottishdance.net; maker pages) for the numbers quoted; grain and selvedge details trade practice.

## Huipil {#huipil}

- **Aliases**: huipile, guipil (Maya regional spellings), blouse (museum catalogues); fr: huipil
- **Family**: Mesoamerican rectangular garments (Mexico, Guatemala)
- **Recognize**: loose sleeveless tunic or blouse made from one to three rectangular woven panels joined along the long edges, with an opening for the head and often side openings left for the arms; frequently heavily embroidered or brocaded at the neck and over the body.
- **Pieces**: loom-width panel (1 to 3, each rectangular), neck facing or binding (optional), side stitching (the sides are left open for arm holes)
- **Shape**: Documented in museum records (Penn Museum; UBC Museum of Anthropology): a huipil is made from one to three panels woven on a backstrap or treadle loom, sewn together lengthwise, often with a decorative joining stitch (randa). With two panels the head opening is often an unsewn slit at the centre seam; with one or three panels, a round, square or V-shaped hole is cut or woven at the centre. Examples: a Nebaj two-panel huipil 42 cm long and 66 cm wide; a Palopo three-panel huipil 72 cm long and 73 cm wide; a Tamahu three-panel huipil 110 cm wide; a San Pedro two-panel huipil 121 cm wide. The neck edge may be left raw, embroidered or appliqued. Regional and community variants differ widely (sleeves, length, whether worn tucked in); the form of each community should be built from its own reference. Source: Penn Museum object records; UBC MOA records.
- **Drafting**: 1. Panel width = loom width (varies by weaver and region; not a single value). 2. Panel count so that width = chest/2 + ease or the desired fullness. 3. Length from shoulder to hip or knee; a long huipil reaches the knee. 4. Neck opening: slit at the join (two panels) or a cut hole of about the head circumference. 5. Side seams: leave the upper part open for the arm.
- **Sewing order**: 1. join panels along the long edges; 2. finish the head opening; 3. sew side seams from the hem up to the arm opening.
- **Finishes and notions**: decorative joining stitch (randa), embroidery, bound neck.
- **Fabrics**: handwoven cotton, wool, silk; commercial cloth in modern versions.
- **Fit faults**: neck opening too small -> enlarge by cutting and rebinding; sides too tight -> raise the arm opening.
- **Variants**: #quechquemitl, #poncho, #kimono (rectangular panels).
- **CLO3D**: build 1-3 rectangles joined at the long edges, cut the neck hole, sew sides leaving an arm gap; cotton preset.
- **Confidence**: documented (museum records) for panel counts and example sizes.

## Chiton (and peplos) {#chiton}

- **Aliases**: Ionic chiton, Doric peplos, Greek tunic, kolpos (the blouse fold); fr: chiton, peplos
- **Family**: Ancient Greek draped garments
- **Recognize**: floor-length garment made from a rectangle of cloth draped on the body, fastened along the shoulders and upper arms with pins (fibulae) or buttons, belted at the waist or below the bust, falling in vertical folds; the peplos (Doric) is a folded-over rectangle with an overfold (apoptygma), the chiton (Ionic) is a tube sewn up the side.
- **Pieces**: rectangle of cloth (1 or 2 joined along the side), belts (1-2 cords), fastenings (pins, fibulae or buttons)
- **Shape**: Documented by the Metropolitan Museum essay on the chiton, peplos and himation: the most common method is stitching two rectangular pieces of cloth together along either side seam, top to bottom, forming a cylinder with the top edge and hem unstitched (or one wide rectangle folded and sewn to a tube). The wearer's arms go out of the openings left between the unstitched top edges, which are fastened at the shoulder by pins or buttons; the chiton is belted, and the length is adjusted by pulling the cloth up over the belt (kolpos). The peplos is one rectangle, folded over at the top, wrapped round the body, pinned at the shoulders, and open on one side. Without surviving garments, art and literature are the only evidence (Met), so dimensions are reconstructions. Typical reenactment sizing: width equals the arm span (or more for fuller folds) and length equals shoulder to floor plus the overfold and kolpos; these are reconstruction practice, unverified. Source: Metropolitan Museum essay (Met); reenactment practice.
- **Drafting**: 1. Measure from shoulder to floor. 2. Measure arm span (fingertip to fingertip) for the width; for a chiton, the width across both panels is arm span plus a margin; for a peplos, width is arm span x 1-1.5 (typical, unverified). 3. Add the overfold depth (apoptygma) typical 20-40 cm for the peplos. 4. Cut as a rectangle with no shaping.
- **Sewing order**: 1. join the side seam(s) of the tube; 2. hem the lower edge; 3. pin the top edge at the shoulders; 4. belt.
- **Finishes and notions**: pins or fibulae, buttons made from small stones or discs, belt cords (zone).
- **Fabrics**: fine linen, wool, silk; a lighter cloth gives the fine pleated Ionic look, wool gives the heavier Doric fall.
- **Fit faults**: bunched at the neck -> reduce the width; slides off the shoulder -> add pin points closer together.
- **Variants**: #himation (outer rectangle), #tunica-roman, #kaftan (T-form compare).
- **CLO3D**: rectangle with pin points; sew a tube; use a thin linen preset; pin by constraining the shoulders; belt as an elastic or a cord.
- **Confidence**: documented in words (Met); dimensions reconstruction practice.

- **Recognize**: T-shape of rectangles, straight sleeves, triangular gores inserted at the sides (and sometimes at the front and back) for hem width, and small square gussets in the underarm for movement.
- **Pieces**: body panel (2, front and back), sleeve (2, rectangles or sleeve-body combined), underarm gusset (2, squares), gore (2-4, triangles), neck facing (1)
- **Shape**: From reenactment and archaeological descriptions. Body: two rectangles (front and back) joined at the shoulders, or one rectangle folded at the shoulders. Sleeves: straight rectangles, either sewn to a straight armhole or cut in one with the body. Underarm gusset: usually a square cut diagonally (diamond) so that the grain runs across the strain; sizes quoted are 5-6 inch squares (about 12-15 cm) in one tutorial and a medieval gusset of about 8-12 cm square in another; the sources agree the gusset is kept as a square (not cut into two triangles). Gores: right-angled triangles added at the side seams for the hem width; one reenactment tutorial uses four triangles about 24 in long and 14 in wide (61 x 36 cm). The Thorsbjerg tunic (just before the Viking Age) is front and back rectangles sewn at the shoulders with sleeves; the neckline is formed by leaving the centre of the shoulder seam unsewn (boat neck). The Herjolfsnes finds (Greenland) have gored bodices: one garment (D10594) is described with four front gores and four back gores with shaped armscyes on the outer pair (secondary source, student synthesis paper); the 1b type of Greenland tunic is dated 1380-1530 by a pattern vendor and the dating differs between sources. Primary pattern drawings: Fransen, Nørgård and Østergård, "Medieval Garments Reconstructed: Norse Clothing Patterns" (Aarhus, 2011); Østergård "Woven into the Earth" (2004); Burnham "Cut My Cote" (Royal Ontario Museum, 1973; contents not read here). Source: reenactment tutorials and secondary sources; drawn patterns not seen.
- **Drafting**: 1. Body width = (chest + ease)/2 per panel plus seam allowances. 2. Length shoulder to knee (tunic) or ankle (kirtle). 3. Sleeve width at the upper arm = bicep + ease. 4. Gusset = square, side = 5-6 in (reported) or 8-12 cm, sewn diagonally. 5. Gores: right-angled triangles with the straight grain along the long side; length from hip down. 6. Neck opening: keyhole or boat neck.
- **Sewing order**: 1. join shoulders; 2. neck facing; 3. sleeves to the body with gussets set into the armhole; 4. gores into the side seams; 5. sew the side seam and sleeve underarm; 6. hem.
- **Finishes and notions**: hand-felled seams, narrow hems, linen thread.
- **Fabrics**: wool (twill), linen; the cloth width limits the panel width, so gores often save fabric.
- **Fit faults**: arms feel tight -> add or enlarge the gusset; hem too narrow to walk -> add gores.
- **Variants**: #cotehardie (fitted), #chiton, #tunica-roman, #historical-shirt-rectangular.
- **CLO3D**: rectangles, gussets as small squares, gores as triangles; add the gusset as a separate piece and sew the corners carefully; wool preset.
- **Confidence**: reenactment practice; archaeological counts from secondary sources.

## Cotehardie (fitted 14th-century gown) {#cotehardie}

- **Aliases**: cote-hardie, kirtle (the under-gown), fitted gown, gown with grande assiette; fr: cotte hardie
- **Family**: Medieval European garments
- **Recognize**: close-fitting bodice and sleeves with a flared skirt, closed by buttons or lacing at front or back, set-in sleeves with a deep armhole, sometimes a tippet (hanging strip) at the elbow.
- **Pieces**: bodice front (2), bodice back (2), side panel (2, gore), skirt gore (4-8), sleeve (2, narrow, fitted), buttons or lacing, tippet (optional)
- **Shape**: Documented in secondary sources: the 14th-century fitted gown is made of long narrow gores in the bodice and skirt so the bodice follows the body; the sleeves are fitted, set into a large armhole by the grande assiette method with triangular gores at the sleeve cap; a tapestry of about 1402 shows back-facing gores. The term cotehardie is disputed: in late 14th/early 15th-century documents it often means a short close-fitting man's overgown, and the buttoned image may be an underdress with buttoned sleeves under a short-sleeved fitted overdress. Closures varied (lacing, eyelets, rings, buttons). The Moy gown (Ireland) has unusual seam placements; its date is uncertain. Dimensions not available. Source: cottesimple.com articles on the grande assiette; reconstructing-history pattern notes; secondary.
- **Drafting**: 1. Draft a close-fitting bodice block (see #close-fitting-bodice-block) with a deep armhole. 2. Add gores to the skirt from the hip or the waist. 3. Sleeve cap: when the sleeve curve is much larger than the armhole, insert a triangular gore at the top of the long seam to spread the curve (reported tip). 4. Closure at front or back; keep the line straight on the grain.
- **Sewing order**: 1. join the bodice panels; 2. add gores; 3. shoulders; 4. sleeves with gores; 5. closure; 6. hem.
- **Finishes and notions**: small covered buttons or lacing, hand-worked buttonholes or eyelets, linen interlining.
- **Fabrics**: wool, linen, silk; fine wool takes the fitted curves.
- **Fit faults**: bodice wrinkles under the arm -> deepen the armhole; skirt twists -> check grain on every gore.
- **Variants**: #medieval-tunic-gored, #houppelande, #dirndl (a surviving folk form).
- **CLO3D**: narrow gores; define the buttons as a separate trim set; wool preset.
- **Confidence**: secondary sources; term and layout disputed; dimensions unverified.

## Houppelande {#houppelande}

- **Aliases**: houpelande, houppelande gown (14th-15th c.); fr: houppelande
- **Family**: Late medieval European overgowns
- **Recognize**: full-length or short gown with a high collar, a close fit at the shoulders and a flare from the shoulders to the hem in many folds (long tubular pleats in the late 15th c.), bag sleeves or wide-cuffed "angel" sleeves, belted at the natural waist or higher.
- **Pieces**: front (1, with pleats), back (1 or 2), sleeve (2, bag or wide), collar (1, stiffened), belt (1), hem trim
- **Shape**: Documented in secondary sources: no separate bodice; fitted at the shoulders, with the body cut as four panels in one modern reconstruction, widening to a flared skirt; the folds fall as pleats or gores; the collar is high and may be stiffened; sleeves are bag-shaped (gathered into a wrist band) or open into wide cuffs. Exact cutting layouts are specific to the garment (the Houppelande of John of Goerlitz is cited as a documented piece in a bibliography). Not found in Arnold's volumes. Source: secondary pages on houppelande construction (encyclopedia entries and reconstructions); drawn pattern not seen. Unverified.
- **Drafting**: 1. Measure shoulder width, length, and height of the collar. 2. Four panels widening toward the hem (reconstruction practice). 3. Gather or pleat the skirt into the waist belt. 4. Make the collar to fit the neck opening (a reconstruction note says collars often need adjusting).
- **Sewing order**: 1. panel seams; 2. shoulder seams; 3. sleeves; 4. collar; 5. lining; 6. hem; 7. pleat and belt.
- **Finishes and notions**: scalloped or dagged edges, fur trim, wire or buckram stiffener in the collar.
- **Fabrics**: wool, velvet, silk brocade; fur lining in the cold.
- **Fit faults**: collar will not sit -> reshape the neck curve; folds collapse -> use heavier cloth or interlining.
- **Variants**: #cotehardie, #medieval-tunic-gored.
- **CLO3D**: panels and gored skirt, pleats by fold lines; heavy wool or velvet preset.
- **Confidence**: from secondary sources; layout unverified.

## Smock / chemise (16th-17th century) {#smock-chemise}

- **Aliases**: shift, chemise, woman's smock, undershirt, "sark" (Scots); fr: chemise
- **Family**: Historical linen undergarments
- **Recognize**: loose linen garment of rectangular and triangular pieces, gathered at the neck into a narrow band, straight or gathered sleeves with underarm gussets, side gores for hem width, worn next to the skin under stays or a gown.
- **Pieces**: body panel (2, front and back), side gore (2-4, triangle), sleeve (2, rectangle), underarm gusset (2, square), neck band (1), cuff (2)
- **Shape**: Documented in Arnold, Patterns of Fashion 4: the cut and construction of linen shirts, smocks, neckwear, headwear and accessories, which gives smock and shirt patterns for the 16th-17th centuries; the general principle of cutting all pieces from rectangles and triangles to avoid waste is confirmed. Typical elements (rectangular body, square underarm gusset, triangular side gores, gathered neck) are documented in reenactment sources for both shirts and smocks; exact dimensions per museum garment were not read. Unverified numerically.
- **Drafting**: 1. Measure length and the width of the cloth. 2. Body: two rectangles, as wide as the cloth allows (one loom width of linen; the width is not known here), minus the overlap of the gores. 3. Gore: right-angled triangle, long side along the grain, length from the underarm to the hem. 4. Sleeve rectangle, wrist to shoulder length. 5. Gusset: square about 10-12 cm. 6. Neck: slit gathered into a narrow band.
- **Sewing order**: 1. join body and gores along the straight grain; 2. neck opening; 3. sleeves with gusset; 4. side seams to the hem; 5. neck band; 6. cuffs; 7. hem.
- **Finishes and notions**: small rolled hems, felled or French seams, linen thread, cord drawstring at the neck.
- **Fabrics**: fine linen, hemp.
- **Fit faults**: arms restricted -> enlarge the gusset; shoulder falls -> narrow the neck band.
- **Variants**: #historical-shirt-rectangular, #medieval-tunic-gored.
- **CLO3D**: rectangles plus triangles; shirr the neck to a band by a fabric pull.
- **Confidence**: documented in Arnold (title) and reenactment practice; dimensions unverified.

## Historical shirt (rectangular construction) {#historical-shirt-rectangular}

- **Aliases**: gusseted shirt, 18th-century man's shirt, linen shirt, shift (women's equivalent); fr: chemise historique à gousset
- **Family**: Historical linen undergarments
- **Recognize**: T-shaped linen shirt made only of rectangles and squares: a long body with no shaping, straight sleeves, a narrow collar band and cuffs, square gussets at the underarm, side and neck, a slit at the neck gathered into the collar, and slits at the hem sides.
- **Pieces**: body (1, front and back as one long rectangle folded at the shoulder), collar (1), sleeve (2), shoulder strap (2), cuff (2), sleeve (underarm) gusset (2), side gusset (2), neck gusset (2)
- **Shape**: Documented by the Victoria and Albert Museum for a British shirt of 1740-1780: T-shaped, constructed of rectangular pieces: body, collar, 2 sleeves, 2 shoulder straps, 2 cuffs, 2 sleeve gussets, 2 side gussets and 2 neck gussets; the cutting was arranged so that no scraps were left over from the length of linen. The same record gives the surviving garment's measurements: chest circumference 162 cm, waist circumference 162 cm, nape-to-hem length 104 cm, across-back width 65 cm, sleeve length 55 cm, cuff circumference 18 cm, shoulder length 23 cm, collar 4 inches deep; an earlier shirt (1697-1702) has a 2.5-inch collar and 1-inch wristbands. A Colonial Williamsburg record (1775-1790, American) gives total length 76.5 in with the sleeves, body length 41.5 in, neck circumference 14.125 in, and explains that the body has no taper: shoulder slope and underarm ease come from square gussets folded diagonally into triangles, and the shoulder fullness is gathered into the collar; shoulders may be reinforced with a T-shaped self-fabric lining; sleeve fullness was sometimes pleated to fit under a coat sleeve. Neck gussets are described by hobbyist sources as folded rectangles in the shoulder seam (not a primary source). Source: V&A shirt records; Colonial Williamsburg record. Arnold, Patterns of Fashion 4 (linen shirts, smocks, neckwear, c.1540-1660) has the earlier shirts (not read here).
- **Drafting**: 1. Start from a surviving garment's dimensions, or use the V&A numbers above for an average man's shirt. 2. Body: a rectangle as wide as the loom or the chosen width, length = shirt length x 2 (folded over the shoulder). 3. Cut the neck slit and a small keyhole at the fold. 4. Sleeve: rectangle = arm length x upper-arm circumference + ease. 5. Gussets: squares, folded or cut diagonally so the grain crosses the strain. 6. Shoulder strap: narrow rectangle (the width of the shoulder seam). 7. Cuff and collar bands: narrow rectangles. All dimension choices not listed above are unverified.
- **Sewing order**: 1. shoulder straps to the body; 2. neck gussets; 3. sleeves with underarm gussets; 4. side seams with side gussets; 5. slits at the hem; 6. collar band; 7. cuffs; 8. hems.
- **Finishes and notions**: flat-felled or whipped seams, rolled hems, small linen buttons or thread-covered buttons, cord at the neck.
- **Fabrics**: linen (fine plain weave), hemp, later cotton; the narrow loom widths determine how many panels make the body.
- **Fit faults**: arm movement is limited -> enlarge the underarm gusset; collar tight -> lengthen the neck slit and re-gather.
- **Variants**: #smock-chemise (women's), #medieval-tunic-gored, #folk-shirt.
- **CLO3D**: build the body as one rectangle folded, gussets as small square pieces; sew from the gusset corner; use a fine linen preset; do the gathering at the collar by shirring or by narrow seam shortening.
- **Confidence**: documented (V&A; Colonial Williamsburg); measurements are of single garments, not a standard.

## 18th-century stays {#stays-18c}

- **Aliases**: stays, corset (modern term), jumps (lighter), pair of bodies (earlier); fr: corps à baleines
- **Family**: Historical foundation garments
- **Recognize**: stiff, boned, conical torso with a straight or slightly lowered front, shoulder straps, tabs at the waist, lacing at the back (and sometimes the front), a flat busk in a front channel, wide straps, covered or bound edges.
- **Pieces**: centre front (2, with a busk pocket), side front (2), side (2), back (2, with lacing eyelets), shoulder strap (2), lining and interlining (matching), edge binding (strips), tabs (integral to the pieces)
- **Shape**: Arnold, Patterns of Fashion 5 (2018, completed by Tiramani and colleagues) gives patterns for 26 pairs of bodies and stays, 2 busks, a stomacher, 10 hoops, a farthingale and a rump from c.1595-1795, with X-rays and colour-coded boning, and distinguishes stitched stays from covered stays. The exact piece count differs from garment to garment. A reconstruction maker following the book layered an outer linen, two layers of linen buckram and a lining, marked boning channels on the flat-lined pieces, covered visible seams with linen tape and bound the edges. Piece list here is the common one (centre front, side front, side, back, straps) from reconstruction practice; per-garment shapes and numbers are unverified in this entry. Source: School of Historical Dress publication details; Quintessential Clothespen reconstruction posts (secondary).
- **Drafting**: 1. Take bust, underbust, waist, back length, front length, shoulder width. 2. Draw a conical torso with a flat or concave front line from the bust to the waist. 3. Divide into front, side front, side and back. 4. Position the boning channels perpendicular or radial to the curve (radial bones near the bust, vertical elsewhere). 5. Add straps set in the back and tied or sewn at the front. 6. Seam allowances 6-12 mm (typical for hand-bound edges). 7. Eyelet spacing: close, 2-3 cm (typical, unverified).
- **Sewing order**: 1. flat-line the pieces with interlining; 2. stitch boning channels; 3. join pieces (seams covered by tape); 4. insert bones and busk; 5. bind the top edge and the tabs; 6. work eyelets; 7. attach straps.
- **Finishes and notions**: hand-bound eyelets, linen tape binding, whalebone, reed or modern synthetic boning, wood or bone busk, lacing cord.
- **Fabrics**: linen, linen buckram, cotton drill, wool; coutil in modern repro.
- **Fit faults**: gaps at the back -> shorten the lacing opening or add width at the side backs; digs at the hip -> reduce the tab depth or re-cut the hip curve; top edge gapes -> add a small dart or lower the top edge.
- **Variants**: #robe-francaise (worn over), #waistcoat-18c, #crinoline-cage.
- **CLO3D**: build as a stiff garment on the avatar shape (use a very high stiffness and thickness, rigid fabric); the boning as fold or internal lines; avatar posture must be corseted by hand.
- **Confidence**: documented by title and make-up (secondary); piece shapes unverified.

## Robe à la française (sack-back gown) {#robe-francaise}

- **Aliases**: sack-back gown, sacque gown, Watteau-back gown, robe volante (earlier); fr: robe à la française
- **Family**: 18th-century European women's gowns
- **Recognize**: gown with box pleats falling from the back neck to the floor (Watteau pleats), open in front over a petticoat, a stomacher in the front opening, robings on the front edges, sleeves ending at the elbow with ruffles (engageantes), worn over stays and wide hoops, often looped up through pocket slits (retroussée dans les poches).
- **Pieces**: back (1, pleated, with lining), front (2, fitted with robings), lining (matching, boned in the back in some forms), robing (2), stomacher (1), sleeve (2), sleeve ruffle (2), petticoat (1, separate)
- **Shape**: Documented in secondary reconstruction writing (Demode Couture; Arnold, Patterns of Fashion 1, which has gridded patterns for sacque back garments): the back is pleated and sewn to a back lining which in one reconstruction is boned and eyelet-adjustable; each front is draped or patterned and fitted, with robings cut separately and sewn on the front line; sleeves are set into a curved armhole with ruffles; the gown skirt is pleated open and fanned to the back; the petticoat is cut separately, side seams first, then pleated to a waistband. Reconstruction notes that Arnold's pattern is mostly straight panels with some pleating and a curved front pleat line; commercial versions often differ by adding a waist seam. Arnold's volume has the original drafts. Not read here. Unverified numerically.
- **Drafting**: 1. Measure shoulder, bust, underbust, length to the floor over hoops, hoop width. 2. Pleat the back: the pleats from the back neck are pressed and stitched at the neck, with fullness depending on the cloth width (not known; take from the museum drawing). 3. Fit the front by draping over stays and hoops. 4. Cut robings. 5. Set the sleeve. 6. Finish with the petticoat.
- **Sewing order**: 1. line and fit the back; 2. fitted front lining; 3. robings; 4. sleeves; 5. trim; 6. petticoat; 7. stomacher.
- **Finishes and notions**: pins at the stomacher, hooks, hand-sewn finishes; decorative trim (pinked or gathered).
- **Fabrics**: silk satin, taffeta, brocade; the folds in the box pleat need a crisp cloth.
- **Fit faults**: back pleats gape at the neck -> shorten the neck line; front gapes -> add or reduce the robing width.
- **Variants**: #regency-gown (later), #stays-18c, #crinoline-cage (later hoop form).
- **CLO3D**: back lining and pleated outer panel; use pleats as fold lines; place a hoop cage under it as a rigid avatar item.
- **Confidence**: secondary sources; shape unverified.

## 18th-century breeches {#breeches-18c}

- **Aliases**: knee breeches, fall-front breeches, fly-front breeches; fr: culotte
- **Family**: Historical men's garments
- **Recognize**: close-fitting trousers ending at or just below the knee, a fall-front (broad flap buttoned at the waist) from about the 1760s-70s, or a fly front earlier; a knee band closed by buttons or a buckle; a waistband with a cord or lacing at the centre back; side pockets.
- **Pieces**: front leg (2), back leg (2), fall (1), waistband (1), knee band (2), pocket bag (2), lining (matching), gusset in the back opening (1)

- **Fabrics**: wool broadcloth, silk, leather, fustian.
- **Fit faults**: seat bags -> shorten the rise at the back; knees pinch -> loosen the band.
- **Variants**: #trunk-hose (earlier), #waistcoat-18c, #mens-trousers (modern).
- **CLO3D**: four main panels; fall as a separate flap; knee band by buttons by hand.
- **Confidence**: documented (V&A; Colonial Williamsburg) for features; dimensions unverified.

## Regency gown (drawstring round gown) {#regency-gown}

- **Aliases**: Empire gown, high-waisted round gown, drop-front gown, bib-front gown, chemise gown; fr: robe Empire
- **Family**: Historical women's gowns (c.1795-1820)
- **Recognize**: high waistline just under the bust, a narrow bodice with a diamond-shaped back (straight back seams from the shoulders), a skirt of rectangles gathered at the back, short puff sleeves, a gathered neckline; the drop-front version has a bib front pinned or tied on.
- **Pieces**: bodice front (1 or 2, gathered into the neck), bodice back (2, diamond-shaped), sleeve (2), skirt front panel (1), skirt back panel (1, slit at the centre back), skirt gore (2, narrow), waistband or tapes (1), lining (matching)
- **Shape**: Documented by a museum record (Irma G. Bowen Historic Clothing Collection, University of New Hampshire) for a gown skirt of four pieces and unlined: one panel each in front and back and two narrow gores; the back panel is slit at the centre and the combined 20.3 cm (8 in) of the back and gore on each side of the slit is tightly gathered into the waistband opening. Maker studies of extant gowns: the bodice front is gathered separately from the skirt so the skirt front stays flat, and the back bodice gathers to match the skirt gathers; the bodice is pulled up by cords run through the neckline and waistline (two drawstrings only). The classic diamond back has straight back seams and dropped shoulder seams; shoulder seams sit behind the shoulder by about 1.5 in. Front panel width depends on date: narrow around 1805-1810, more flare by the 1810s. Construction page numbers in Arnold, Patterns of Fashion 1: pp. 48-49 and 52 (drawstring construction). Source: UNH record; sensibility.com; Fashion Through Her Story (reconstruction); Arnold PoF 1 (indirectly).
- **Drafting**: 1. Measure bust, underbust, back length, shoulder width, skirt length. 2. Back bodice: draw two pieces, join to form a diamond; straight seams from the armhole to the waist. 3. Front panel: rectangle at the underbust, gathered into the neck cord. 4. Skirt: four pieces (front panel, back panel, two gores), the front width taken across the underbust (reported advice). 5. Sleeve: short puff.
- **Sewing order**: 1. join the back pieces; 2. gather the front; 3. shoulder seams; 4. side seams; 5. line the bodice; 6. skirt pieces; 7. gather the skirt back; 8. waistband tapes; 9. sleeves.
- **Finishes and notions**: drawstring tapes, hooks and eyes or buttons at the back, linen lining for the bodice.
- **Fabrics**: fine muslin, cotton lawn, silk; sheers need a slip.
- **Fit faults**: neckline gapes -> tighten the drawstring; waistline rides up -> lengthen the back piece.
- **Variants**: #robe-francaise (earlier), #victorian-bodice (later), #smock-chemise (slip).
- **CLO3D**: diamond-back bodice, rectangular skirt panels with gathering; shirr at the neck and waist; muslin preset.
- **Confidence**: documented in a museum record and maker studies; dimensions unverified.

## Victorian bodice (cuirass and day bodice) {#victorian-bodice}

- **Aliases**: bodice, basque, cuirass bodice, dinner bodice, polonaise bodice; fr: corsage Victorien
- **Family**: Historical women's garments (c.1840-1900)
- **Recognize**: close-fitting, boned bodice cut with many seams, high or low neckline, set-in sleeves, front buttons or hooks; the cuirass bodice (1875-1890) is long-waisted, extending over the hips with a point or a longer square tail at the back.
- **Pieces**: front (2, with short close darts), side front (2), side back (2), back (2), sleeve (2, two-piece), collar (1), boning strips, lining (matching), facing (2)
- **Shape**: Documented by Penelope Byrde (as quoted in a fashion history source): the cuirass bodice was cut with five seams at the back, from the top of the shoulder slanting toward the waist; the front darts were short and close together; the bodice was well boned inside so it stayed smooth; some extended past the hip and had a longer square tail in the back. Truly Victorian #422 (1881 dinner bodice) is fitted snugly over a corset without a bustle and has a two-piece sleeve; #460B (1885 bustle cuirass) has a higher cut shoulder. Piece counts above are from pattern descriptions, not a drawn extant pattern. Source: FIT fashion history (cuirass bodice); Truly Victorian listings; Arnold, Patterns of Fashion 2 (1860-1940) has the extant drafts (not read).
- **Drafting**: 1. Fit the bodice over the corset (and bustle). 2. Draft a close-fitting block with front darts at the bust and side-front/side-back seams shaped from the armhole to the waist. 3. Extend over the hip for the cuirass length. 4. Position five back seams (centre back, two side-back seams, two sides). 5. Boning at every seam, plus the darts.
- **Sewing order**: 1. line and bone the pieces; 2. join seams; 3. shoulder; 4. sleeves; 5. neckline and collar; 6. closure; 7. hem.
- **Finishes and notions**: hooks and eyes or buttons, bone casing on each seam, bound hem.
- **Fabrics**: silk, wool, cotton sateen; lining of cotton.
- **Fit faults**: wrinkles at the front waist -> shorten the dart and add boning; the back pulls up -> lengthen the cuirass tail.
- **Variants**: #bustle, #crinoline-cage, #regency-gown, #close-fitting-bodice-block.
- **CLO3D**: build bodice with princess-style seams; use a stiffer fabric preset; the corset shape comes from a rigid avatar.
- **Confidence**: documented in words (Byrde; pattern notes); shape unverified numerically.

## Crinoline cage (hoop skirt) {#crinoline-cage}

- **Aliases**: cage crinoline, hoop skirt, cage, steel crinoline; fr: crinoline, cage
- **Family**: Historical foundation garments (c.1856-1870)
- **Recognize**: dome-shaped cage of steel hoops of increasing diameter, hung from vertical tapes, with a waistband; no petticoat fullness; the shape is a cone or dome.
- **Pieces**: waistband (1), vertical tape (4-8), horizontal hoop (many; counts below), hoop casing or loop on the tape (as many as hoops), closure (hooks or ties)
- **Shape**: Documented in museum catalogues: spring-steel hoops of increasing diameter toward the hem, held by cotton tapes. Counts of hoops vary: a Maidstone Museum cage (late 1850s) has 20 hoops hung on twill cotton tape, with a bottom circumference of over 9 ft (about 293 cm); a Newport cage has 20 complete circles and 2 U-shaped hoops near the waist; V&A: 9-18 hoops according to formality; a University of New Hampshire cage (1868-1873) has 9 wire semicircles hip to hip around the back at the top and 20 round hoops beneath; V&A "A Favorite of the Empress" cage has a round bottom circumference of 235 cm; secondary sources give widest circumference up to six yards (549 cm). Hoops pass through stitched openings in the vertical tapes or are fastened to them with rivets or clamps. A modern reconstruction tutorial spaces the hoops 10-15 cm apart (not period). Source: Maidstone, V&A, UNH and Newport museum records; Wikipedia "Crinoline".
- **Drafting**: 1. Hem circumference from the style (examples above). 2. Number of hoops (9-20). 3. Hoop circumferences increase gradually; the largest hoop at the hem. 4. Tape length = waist to hem plus allowance. 5. Spacing about 10-15 cm in a modern reconstruction (not period). 6. Waistband length = waist plus overlap.
- **Sewing order**: 1. waistband; 2. vertical tapes; 3. hoop openings; 4. thread the hoops; 5. join the hoop ends with a clasp; 6. closure.
- **Finishes and notions**: spring steel (or flat steel or reed or modern flexible boning), cotton tapes, hooks.
- **Fabrics**: twill tape, cotton, sometimes a fabric-covered cage.
- **Fit faults**: cage tilts or flips -> shift the front hoops forward and shorten the front tapes; hoop spacing too wide -> a uneven skirt line.
- **Variants**: #bustle (later), #robe-francaise (earlier hoop form: paniers).
- **CLO3D**: hoops as rigid cone shells or separate loops; no cloth cage simulation; place the dress above it with collision on.
- **Confidence**: documented (museum records); modern spacing from a tutorial.

## Bustle (1870s-1880s) {#bustle}

- **Aliases**: crinolette, bustle pad, bustle cage, dress improver; fr: tournure
- **Family**: Historical foundation garments and skirts (c.1869-1890)
- **Recognize**: skirt support pushing fullness to the back at the waist, either a separate pad or cage or a foundation skirt with steel ribs built in, giving a shelf at the back of the dress.
- **Pieces**: waistband (1), bustle pad or cage (1), foundation skirt panel (gored, 4-6), back rectangle (1), steel or flat steel ribs (3-8), tapes (4-6)
- **Shape**: Documented in secondary and museum sources: early bustles were separate structures attached around the waist and included crinolettes of steel half-hoops, down-filled pads and wire mesh; by about 1885 the bustle was often built into the foundation skirt, with a small pad attached to the waistband with horizontal rows of steel that could be pulled into a curved shape. A foundation skirt reconstructed from Arnold, Patterns of Fashion 2 is a set of gored panels with angled edges toward the back and a rectangular back panel over the bustle. Source: V&A and secondary sources; Arnold PoF 2 via a maker. Numbers unverified.
- **Drafting**: 1. Waist, back length, hem circumference. 2. Gored front and side panels. 3. Rectangular back panel with horizontal rib casings at spacing set by the desired shelf (not known). 4. Draw the ribs shorter at the top and longer at the bottom.
- **Sewing order**: 1. foundation skirt panels; 2. rib casings; 3. insert ribs; 4. waistband; 5. tapes that pull the skirt backward.
- **Finishes and notions**: flat steel, tapes, hooks, waist ribbons.
- **Fabrics**: cotton drill, calico, horsehair braid.
- **Fit faults**: shelf too high or low -> adjust the position of the first rib; skirt rides up -> shorten the tapes.
- **Variants**: #crinoline-cage (earlier), #victorian-bodice.
- **CLO3D**: skirt with rigid ribs (stiff internal lines or separate pieces); simulate the dress over it.
- **Confidence**: secondary sources; dimensions unverified.

## Frock coat {#frock-coat}

- **Aliases**: Prince Albert coat (double-breasted), surtout, redingote; fr: redingote
- **Family**: Historical men's tailoring (c.1820-1910)
- **Recognize**: knee-length coat with a waist seam, a full skirt in front and behind, fitted torso, collar and lapels, single or double breasted, back vent, skirt cut straight across the front.
- **Pieces**: forepart (2), side body (2), back (2), skirt front (2), skirt back (2), sleeve (2, two-piece), collar (1), lapel facing (2), pocket flap (2), lining (matching)
- **Shape**: Documented in the Victoria and Albert Museum record cited by a search summary: tailors introduced the waist seam in the 1820s; frock coats, dress coats, morning coats and tailcoats share the waist seam construction and differ in the cut of their skirts. Frock coat skirts are cut straight across the front (not cut away). Assembly sequence from a historical frock coat reconstruction (Historical Tailoring Masterclasses): side bodies sewn to the forepart first, then the skirt to the forepart; the back is joined last, easing in fullness in the side body; the back skirt is made with a facing and a lining. Full drafting geometry not obtained. Source: V&A record; historical-tailoring.com; Wikipedia "Frock coat".
- **Drafting**: 1. Coat block with waist seam (see #coat-block and #jacket-block). 2. Skirt width at the waist = the width of the body at the waist plus fullness; hem width wider than the waist. 3. Pleats or vent at the centre back. 4. Collar with notch lapels.
- **Sewing order**: 1. canvas the forepart; 2. sew side bodies; 3. join skirt to the waist seam; 4. join back; 5. collar; 6. sleeves; 7. lining.
- **Finishes and notions**: hair canvas, wadding in the chest, bound buttonholes, silk lining, covered buttons.
- **Fabrics**: wool broadcloth, worsted, cavalry twill; silk facing.
- **Fit faults**: the skirt flares at the front -> shorten the skirt front; drag lines at the waist -> adjust the waist-seam position.
- **Variants**: #tailcoat, #suit-jacket-single-breasted, #double-breasted-jacket.
- **CLO3D**: build the upper body and the skirt as two pieces joined at the waist seam; wool preset.
- **Confidence**: documented (V&A; tailoring pages); geometry unverified.

## Tailcoat (dress coat) {#tailcoat}

- **Aliases**: dress coat, swallowtail coat, white tie coat, morning coat (related); fr: habit à queue
- **Family**: Historical and formal men's tailoring
- **Recognize**: waist-length coat in front (cut away in a horizontal line at the waist) and tails behind to the knee, a waist seam, peaked or shawl lapels, two-piece sleeves, a split back between the tails.
- **Pieces**: forepart (2), side front (2), centre back (2), side back (2), tail (2), sleeve (2, two-piece), collar (1), lapel facing (2), pocket welt or flap (2), lining (matching)
- **Shape**: Documented in a modern commercial pattern description (Minerva Patterns): the cutting plan is centre back, side back, side front, centre front panels, with tail pieces; the collar, flaps, pocket welts and two-piece sleeves are cut separately; the tails are attached to the side backs (seams pressed open); shaped seams join each centre panel to its side panel; labels vary between pattern makers. V&A: tailors introduced the waist seam in the 1820s; by the early 19th century fashionable coats were completely cut away in front. Frock coats, tailcoats and morning coats share the waist seam construction; only the cut of the skirts differs. Source: Minerva Patterns descriptions; V&A record (via search summary). The geometry of the cutaway curve and the tail shape was not found. Unverified.
- **Drafting**: Not known here for the cutaway curve. Use a tailcoat block from a tailoring text (see #coat-block and #dresdner-tailoring; Rundschau/Dresdner systems cover formal wear).
- **Sewing order**: 1. canvas the front; 2. side bodies; 3. waist seam and tails; 4. back; 5. collar and lapels; 6. sleeves; 7. lining.
- **Finishes and notions**: silk-faced lapels, a canvas front, bound buttonholes.
- **Fabrics**: barathea, wool worsted, silk facing.
- **Fit faults**: tails flare -> narrow them at the waist seam; front sits open -> add chest overlap.
- **Variants**: #frock-coat, #tuxedo-dinner-jacket, #suit-jacket-single-breasted.
- **CLO3D**: separate upper body and tails, sew at the waist seam; wool preset.
- **Confidence**: documented in pattern descriptions; geometry unverified.

## 1920s drop-waist dress {#dress-1920s-drop-waist}

- **Aliases**: flapper dress, chemise dress, tubular dress, low-waisted dress; fr: robe années 20
- **Family**: Historical women's dresses (c.1920-1929)
- **Recognize**: straight, loose silhouette with the waistline at the hip level (a seam, a sash or a hip band), a skirt that is straight, pleated, gathered or gored, a simple bodice with no darts or soft darts, sleeveless or with a kimono or set-in sleeve, and a neckline that is bateau, V or round.
- **Pieces**: bodice front (1), bodice back (1), hip yoke or band (1-2), skirt front (1), skirt back (1), sleeve (2 or kimono cut), facings (2)
- **Shape**: From period pattern descriptions (Past Patterns reproductions): sheath or slip-on dresses with a dropped hip-level waistline; hip yokes and attached pointed hip bands are typical of the mid-1920s; some patterns use a one-piece yoke joined to a straight front and back, with plaits (pleats) at the sides. Seam positions are not given in the sources read; a modern sloper tutorial puts the hip line 21-23 cm below the waist (modern method, not period). The tubular body is a straight rectangle with minimal shaping. Unverified numerically.
- **Drafting**: 1. Use a straight dress block with no waist dart (see #dress-block). 2. Drop the waist seam to the hip (about 18-23 cm below the natural waist, typical; verify on the original pattern). 3. Gather, pleat or plait the skirt into the hip band or yoke. 4. Keep the bodice loose: ease at the bust and hip.
- **Sewing order**: 1. shoulders; 2. neckline facing; 3. hip yoke/waist seam; 4. skirt pleats; 5. side seams; 6. armhole facing; 7. hem.
- **Finishes and notions**: side placket with snaps, hooks and eyes, beadwork, pintucks.
- **Fabrics**: crepe de chine, silk georgette, beaded chiffon, jersey; bias-cut fabric changes the hang.
- **Fit faults**: hip pulls -> let out the side seam from the waist; bodice rides up -> lengthen the bodice.
- **Variants**: #dress-block, #yoke-skirt (skirt with a hip yoke), #circle-skirt-petticoat-1950s (later).
- **CLO3D**: straight tube bodice with a hip seam; light chiffon or crepe preset.
- **Confidence**: documented in words (reproduction listings); numbers unverified.

## 1950s circle skirt with petticoat {#circle-skirt-petticoat-1950s}

- **Aliases**: full-circle skirt with crinoline petticoat, poodle skirt, rock and roll skirt, can-can petticoat; fr: jupon corolle
- **Family**: Historical women's skirts (c.1947-1960)
- **Recognize**: full circle skirt falling from a fitted waist to a wide hem, standing out over a stiff petticoat; the waist is small, the hem sweep large.
- **Pieces**: circle skirt (1 or 2 half circles with side seams), waistband (1), petticoat tier (3-5 rectangles or circles), petticoat yoke (1)
- **Shape**: The skirt is a full circle or half-circle with a waist hole at the centre: radius r = waist / (2 x pi), computed with a small ease; see #full-circle-skirt, #half-circle-skirt and #three-quarter-circle-skirt for the drafting. The petticoat is a set of gathered rectangular tiers (tulle, net or stiffened organza) sewn to a yoke at the waist; the tiers get longer and wider toward the bottom. Source: standard circle-skirt geometry; the tier counts and proportions are reconstruction practice and unverified.
- **Drafting**: 1. Waist radius r = waist/(2 x pi) (plus 1-2 cm ease). 2. Skirt length L from the waist hole. 3. Petticoat tiers: ratio about 1.5-2x gathering per tier (typical, unverified). 4. The petticoat is 2-5 cm shorter than the skirt (typical). 5. The petticoat's top yoke fits the hip.
- **Sewing order**: 1. circle skirt: cut waist and hem arcs; 2. side seam with zip; 3. waistband; 4. hem (let it hang 24 hours first); 5. petticoat tiers gathered and joined; 6. yoke.
- **Finishes and notions**: stay-tape at the waist, side zip, horsehair braid at the hem for body.
- **Fabrics**: cotton poplin, polished cotton, felt, taffeta; net or tulle for the petticoat.
- **Fit faults**: hem uneven (bias drop) -> hang before hemming and trim level; skirt stands too wide -> reduce the petticoat tiers.
- **Variants**: #full-circle-skirt, #half-circle-skirt, #three-quarter-circle-skirt, #double-circle-skirt, #flared-skirt.
- **CLO3D**: circle skirt as a ring pattern; set a high pressure or underskirt as a separate stiff layer; run a long simulation before judging the hem.
- **Confidence**: geometry exact; petticoat proportions from memory.
